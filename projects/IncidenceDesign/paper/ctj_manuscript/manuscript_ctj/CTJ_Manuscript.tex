\newcommand{\CTJAbstract}{%
\textbf{Background/Aims:} In spatial cluster randomized trials, treatment effects can spill over and contaminate clusters that neighbor treated clusters, so the choice of which clusters to treat affects how accurately the treatment effect can be estimated. We compared six treatment assignment designs and simple random sampling (SRS) on two criteria, the average estimation error and how large the error can be for the particular assignment a trial happens to draw (allocation risk). We then used the designs in a case study that recommends a design for a statewide extension of a sudden unexpected death (SUD) prevention education pilot trial in North Carolina.

\textbf{Methods:} We simulated trials on a $10\times10$ grid of 100 clusters, varying incidence heterogeneity, spatial dependence, spillover magnitude, whether spillover reached only untreated neighbors or neighbors in both arms (spillover regime) and treatment-effect size. We estimated the treatment effect with a spatial autoregressive lag model fit by maximum likelihood and measured accuracy by mean squared error (MSE). The case study used observed 2018--2021 SUD incidence in 58 North Carolina community-college service-area clusters to decide which clusters were treated, and it simulated a continuous education outcome that does not depend on incidence. Neighbors were defined by queen contiguity in the primary analysis. Sensitivity analyses used rook contiguity, let incidence shift the outcome, and ordered regions by alternative incidence summaries.

\textbf{Results:} The spillover regime determined which designs performed best. On the grid with treatment effect $\beta=1$, Incidence-Guided Saturation, which treats larger shares of clusters in higher-incidence regions, had a control-only mean MSE of 0.135, compared with 0.215 for SRS, while under both-arms spillover no design was detectably more accurate than SRS. In the North Carolina case study, Incidence-Guided Saturation lowered control-only mean MSE by 21.1--29.9\% relative to SRS, depending on the year, and its worst-performing assignments also had lower error than those of SRS. Under both-arms spillover, SRS and several randomized designs had similar accuracy. Across the six designs and SRS in the case study, 95\% $t$-based confidence intervals with a degrees-of-freedom correction covered the true effect 94.2--94.9\% of the time, compared with 92.7--93.6\% for standard Wald intervals.

\textbf{Conclusions:} When spillover is expected mainly in control clusters, Incidence-Guided Saturation had the lowest or near-lowest estimation error and lower allocation risk than SRS. It also gave the highest-burden region the largest treated share. When spillover reaches both arms, SRS remains competitive. Design choice should also weigh model assumptions, the number of clusters treated and the share of the population they contain, and whether enough clusters are available for adequate power. This study evaluates trial designs, not the effectiveness of an implemented intervention.

\section{Introduction}
Cluster randomized trials assign groups rather than individuals when interventions operate through clinics, schools or geographic areas.\cite{donner_design_2000,hayes_cluster_2017} Spatial interference violates the assumption that a cluster's outcome depends only on its own treatment. In practice, adjacent populations can share services, information or environmental exposures, so treating one cluster can change outcomes in its neighbors.\cite{hudgens_toward_2008,jarvis_spatial_2017} Two allocations with the same number of treated clusters can then provide very different information about the treatment effect.\cite{baird_optimal_2018,leung_rateoptimal_2022,walther_spatialcrt_2026}

Design-stage methods, including covariate-constrained randomization, randomized saturation, and independent-set and spatial cluster designs, serve different objectives.\cite{crisp_covariate-constrained_2023,baird_optimal_2018,cai_independentset_2023,leung_rateoptimal_2022} Balancing measured incidence does not ensure that the effect of treatment can be separated from the effect of treated neighbors, and fully alternating treatment can make the two inseparable. For example, in a checkerboard design with neighbors defined by shared edges, every neighbor of a treated cluster is a control and every neighbor of a control is treated. A cluster's share of treated neighbors is then fixed by its own arm, and the treatment effect cannot be separated from the spillover effect.\cite{walther_spatialcrt_2026}

In previous work on small grids, we compared simple random sampling (SRS), meaning complete randomization of a fixed number of clusters, with block stratified sampling, an alternating allocation in which no two clusters that share an edge are in the same arm.\cite{walther_spatialcrt_2026} We refer to this design here as Checkerboard. In that study, we judged each design by its average estimation accuracy and by the risk that the single allocation a trial draws gives a poor estimate. We extend the question to heterogeneous incidence, a larger study region, more designs and an irregular observed geography. In this paper, we compare six designs and SRS on the mean squared error (MSE) of the treatment-effect estimate and on how that error varies across possible assignments.

In this paper, we also use a case study, a simulation based on observed incidence, to recommend a design for a statewide extension of a pilot trial of education for healthcare professionals involved in sudden unexpected death (SUD) prevention. We keep incidence-guided allocation separate from the simulated education outcome, because historical incidence can identify areas of need without implying that education outcomes depend on incidence. Incidence can direct resources toward areas of greater historical burden, balance that burden between arms, or vary the share of clusters treated in each region (saturation). None of these goals ensures that treatment can be separated from neighboring exposure. A trial draws only one assignment. Therefore, we evaluated both the average accuracy of each strategy and whether the design with the lowest average error also protects against an unfavorable allocation.

\section{Methods}
\subsection{Outcome model, estimation and allocation risk}
Clusters are linked by a row-standardized spatial weights matrix $\mathbf{W}$ with elements $w_{ij}=1/d_i$ when clusters $i$ and $j$ are neighbors and $w_{ij}=0$ otherwise, where $d_i$ is the number of neighbors of cluster $i$. For a treatment indicator vector $\mathbf{Z}$, $(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{Z})_i$ is then the proportion of cluster $i$'s neighbors that are treated, an exposure mapping.\cite{aronow_estimating_2017,forastiere_identification_2021} Outcomes followed the spatial autoregressive (SAR) lag model\cite{ord_estimation_1975}
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{Y}=(\mathbf{I}-\rho \mathbf{W})^{-1}[\beta \mathbf{Z}+\psi\,\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{Z})+\tau \mathbf{X}+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}],
\label{eq:grid}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{Y}$ is the vector of cluster outcomes, $\mathbf{X}$ is cluster-level incidence, $\mathbf{I}$ is the identity matrix and $\rho$ is the spatial dependence coefficient. The noise $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ is independent standard normal. Therefore, the intervention effect $\beta$ is in units of its standard deviation. It is a direct effect, the effect of a cluster's own treatment before outcomes spread between neighbors through $(\mathbf{I}-\rho \mathbf{W})^{-1}$, rather than a total effect. Spillover $\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{Z})$ followed one of two regimes, $\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{Z})=\mathbf{W}\mathbf{Z}$ when exposure reaches clusters in both arms and $\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{Z})=(\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{Z})\circ \mathbf{W}\mathbf{Z}$ when it reaches control clusters only. Here $\psi$ is the spillover magnitude and $\circ$ is the elementwise product, which multiplies the two vectors cluster by cluster. Control-only spillover is classical contamination, in which untreated clusters benefit from treated neighbors. Under both-arms spillover, treated clusters also gain from treated neighbors. Spillover here is proportional to the share of treated neighbors, whereas spillover in our earlier study was binary, either present or absent.\cite{walther_spatialcrt_2026} SI Section 15.4 compares proportional, count and binary forms in the case study. The incidence coefficient $\tau$ sets how strongly incidence shifts the outcome, holding treatment and spillover fixed. With $\tau=0$, incidence guides only the allocation, as in the case study. With $\tau>0$, clusters with higher incidence have higher outcomes, as on the grid.

We estimated $\beta$ by maximum likelihood (ML).\cite{bivand_comparing_2015} The fitted SAR lag model included an intercept, treatment, the true regime-specific spillover covariate $\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{Z})$ with $\psi$ estimated, and, in the grid study, incidence. This fit assumes that the analyst knows the form of spillover, so a grid sensitivity analysis refit the model without the spillover term. We report 95\% confidence intervals $\hat\beta \pm q\,\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}\sqrt{N/(N-p)}$, where $\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}$ is the ML standard error, $N$ the number of clusters, $p$ the number of estimated coefficients including $\rho$, and $q = t_{0.975,\,N-p}$. The factor $\sqrt{N/(N-p)}$ replaces the ML variance divisor $N$ with $N-p$, a standard small-sample correction,\cite{mackinnon_heteroskedasticity_1985} although in the spatial lag model this rescaling is approximate and was chosen by calibration. The $t$ quantile allows for the small number of clusters.\cite{leyrat_cluster_2018} In SI Section 15.2, this interval covered $\beta$ more reliably than the Wald interval,\cite{lee_asymptotic_2004} which uses $q = z_{0.975} = 1.96$ without the factor. Accuracy was measured by the mean squared error (MSE), the average of $(\hat\beta-\beta)^2$ over simulated trials, and coverage was measured as the proportion of 95\% confidence intervals containing $\beta$.

With rook contiguity, Checkerboard gives $\mathbf{W}\mathbf{Z}=\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{Z}$, which means $\beta$ is not identifiable and the treatment coefficient estimates $\beta-\psi$. With queen contiguity, $\beta$ is identifiable under Checkerboard but can be estimated imprecisely. Every interior cluster has exactly half its neighbors treated, which makes the control-only spillover covariate a linear function of treatment except at the grid boundary.

For each design, we also asked how large the MSE can be for a particular assignment. For an assignment $a$ and fixed incidence $\mathbf{X}$, the assignment-specific MSE $m(a)=E_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}[(\hat\beta-\beta)^2\mid a,\mathbf{X}]$ is the MSE expected over repeated draws of the outcome noise $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ with the assignment held fixed. A design's MSE is the expectation of $m(a)$ over the design's assignment distribution. Across a design's sampled assignments, we summarized allocation risk, the upper tail of $m(a)$, by the mean of the worst 10\% of assignments (worst-decile mean). We estimated each $m(a)$ by averaging squared errors over many simulated outcomes, to limit the influence of outcome noise on the ranking of assignments.

\subsection{Simulation study}
The simulated study region was a $10\times10$ grid of 100 clusters. Neighbors were clusters sharing an edge or a corner (queen contiguity) in the primary analysis and clusters sharing an edge (rook contiguity) in a sensitivity analysis.\cite{bivand_applied_2008} The simulation crossed $\rho=0,0.01,0.2,0.5$, $\psi=0.5,0.6,0.7,0.8$, both spillover regimes, and $\beta=0.8,1,1.5,2,3$. The investigator specifies $\tau$ according to whether incidence is expected to shift the outcome. It is not estimated at the design stage, and we set $\tau=1$ in the grid simulation. Incidence ranges from 0 to 1, and this value raises the outcome by one noise standard deviation from the lowest to the highest incidence before spatial spread. That shift equals the primary intervention effect $\beta=1$. Treatment that follows incidence is then partly confounded with it, the harder case for designs that use incidence. An incidence configuration is the model that generates incidence over the grid, and an incidence surface is one draw from it. The five configurations were independent Uniform$(0,1)$ incidence, spatial incidence at $\rho_X=0.2$ and $0.5$, and Poisson incidence at the same two values of $\rho_X$. Spatial incidence was $\mathbf{X}=\Phi\{(\mathbf{I}-\rho_X\mathbf{W})^{-1}\mathbf{u}\}$, with $\mathbf{u}$ independent standard normal and $\Phi$ the standard normal distribution function. Poisson death counts were drawn with log-rates $\log(35/100{,}000)+(\mathbf{I}-\rho_X\mathbf{W})^{-1}\mathbf{u}$ and 100,000 people per cluster. The base rate of 35 per 100,000 lies between published United States SUD rates for working-age men and women.\cite{mirzaei_years_2019} The counts were then rank-scaled to $(r_i-0.5)/100$, where $r_i$ is cluster $i$'s rank with average ranks for ties, to put them on the same $(0,1)$ scale as the other configurations, so that one value of $\tau$ shifts the outcome by the same amount from the lowest to the highest incidence in every configuration. These are general simulation settings. The North Carolina data enter only the case study. For each configuration we generated ten independent incidence surfaces, each shared by every parameter setting and design. Within surface $k$, the assignments, the simulated outcomes and the fitted model all used the same incidence $\mathbf{X}_k$.

Each scenario combined one parameter setting with one design and had 250 fits, from ten surfaces with 25 assignment and outcome draws per surface. Designs compared under the same parameter setting shared surfaces and outcome noise, and the treatment-effect levels reused the same assignments and noise. Each performance metric is an average over a finite number of simulated trials, and its Monte Carlo standard error (MCSE) measures the simulation error in that average.\cite{morris_using_2019} MCSEs and paired comparisons between designs used the 50 independent configuration-by-surface units (five configurations by ten surfaces), rather than treating settings that share a surface as independent replications.

We also ran a separate allocation-risk study on the grid, using all 50 configuration-by-surface units with queen contiguity, $\beta=1$, $\rho\in\{0,0.5\}$ and $\psi\in\{0.5,0.8\}$. For each design we drew 100 assignments per surface and setting and simulated 100 outcomes for each distinct assignment.

\subsection{Case study: sudden unexpected death in North Carolina}
We aggregated corrected county-year SUD counts prepared by Habib\cite{habib_temporal_2026} and Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results (SEER) population estimates for ages 18--64\cite{seer_population_data} into 58 community-college service-area clusters that partition the 100 counties of North Carolina (NC). SUD deaths were identified from deidentified death certificates (see Ethical considerations) with the case-adjudication algorithm of Gan et al.,\cite{gan_factors_2019} giving 23,523 deaths over 25,594,321 person-years during 2018--2021. Observed incidence and geography were fixed inputs, and outcomes were simulated (Figure~\ref{fig:nc-map}). The four years were analyzed as separate planning scenarios rather than as follow-up of an intervention.
\ifctjreading
\CTJMapFigure
\fi

The primary outcome represented a continuous cluster-level education outcome measured in healthcare professionals. It followed Equation~(\ref{eq:grid}) with $\tau=0$, $\beta=1$ and the row-standardized queen contiguity matrix of the 58 clusters. The education outcome is not expected to depend on SUD incidence. Incidence guided only the allocation, and $\tau=1$ served as a hypothetical sensitivity analysis.

We adapted each design to the irregular map (Table~\ref{tab:allocation-rules}). Incidence-Guided Saturation used four fixed geographic regions. We ran $k$-means clustering on the cluster coordinates 250 times and kept the partition that best balanced compactness, population, number of clusters and number of counties. The regions received saturations of 80\%, 60\%, 40\% and 20\% in order of the mean rank of their clusters' annual rates. $2\times2$ Blocking became spatial blocking. Each year, $k$-means clustering formed 14 groups of similar nearby clusters from standardized location, that year's incidence rank and log population, and a random half of each group was treated (rounding in SI Section 5.2). Therefore, treatment budgets differed between designs (Table~\ref{tab:allocation-rules}). Rather than equalizing budgets after the fact, we report each design's number of treated clusters and its share of the working-age population, a population distinct from the healthcare professionals whose outcomes would be measured.

Primary queen settings crossed $\rho\in\{0,0.2,0.5\}$ with $\psi\in\{0.5,0.8\}$, both regimes, the four years, the six designs, the two Table~\ref{tab:allocation-rules} variants and SRS. One sensitivity analysis let incidence shift the simulated outcome ($\tau=1$) and added incidence as a covariate in the fitted model. Others used rook contiguity at $\rho\in\{0,0.5\}$ and $\psi\in\{0.5,0.8\}$ or ordered the saturation regions by mean raw rates or pooled regional rates instead of mean rank (SI Section 14).

For each setting we drew 100 assignments and simulated 100 independent outcomes for each distinct assignment, using 1,000 outcomes for designs with only one possible assignment. A prespecified rule increased replication until the MCSE of mean MSE was at most 5\% of its value and the MCSE of coverage was at most 0.01. These MCSEs accounted for both assignment and outcome variation. For allocation risk at $\rho\in\{0,0.5\}$ and $\psi=0.8$, we simulated 400 outcomes per assignment and checked the worst decile by selecting it with half of these outcomes and evaluating it with the other half. Finally, we repeated the case study with random subsets of 15 to 58 participating colleges to estimate the number needed for 80\% power, with $\rho=0.5$ and spillover proportional to the effect ($\psi=0.8\beta$). We rejected $\beta=0$ when the 95\% $t$-based interval excluded zero, a two-sided test at level 0.05 (SI Section 5.5), whereas the grid power in SI Section 4 is one-sided at level 0.025.

\section{Results}
\subsection{Simulation study: the spillover regime determines which designs perform best}
Table~\ref{tab:grid-srs} reports the queen grid benchmark at $\beta=1$. The two spillover regimes gave opposite results. Under control-only spillover, Incidence-Guided Saturation's MSE was 0.135 against 0.215 for SRS, a ratio of 0.63. Under both-arms spillover it was 0.046 against 0.042, a ratio of 1.08 and a small but detectable disadvantage (paired interval excluding 1). No design was detectably more accurate than SRS under both-arms spillover, and Balanced Quartiles did not differ detectably from SRS in either regime. Coverage was 94--95\% for every design whose effect was identifiable, while under rook contiguity Checkerboard's was not identifiable and its estimate was substantially biased. Incidence configuration changed each design's MSE by less than 10\%, except for High Incidence Focus, and did not change which design had the lowest MSE under control-only spillover (Figure~\ref{fig:grid}).

The regime mattered most for interspersed assignments. Checkerboard MSE rose from 0.062 under both-arms spillover to 2.111 under control-only spillover, and $2\times2$ Blocking MSE rose from 0.062 to 0.576. Their worse control-only accuracy reflects imprecision rather than bias (SI Figure S8). For Isolation Buffer, the two regimes define the same model because no treated cluster has a treated neighbor, and its small differences between regimes are Monte Carlo error.

The control-only advantage of Incidence-Guided Saturation narrowed at larger effects. At $\beta=2$, Isolation Buffer and Incidence-Guided Saturation had nearly equal MSE (0.160 and 0.160). At $\beta=3$, Isolation Buffer was lower (0.160 versus 0.182), because it largely avoids the error in estimating $\rho$ that for most designs passes into $\hat\beta$ in proportion to $\beta$ (SI Section 6).

When the fitted model omitted the spillover term, every design whose effect was identifiable underestimated $\beta$. At $\beta=1$, bias ranged from $-0.121$ to $-0.400$ and $t$-based coverage was 54--91\% across designs and contiguity types. Some designs still had lower MSE than under the correct model, because their reduced variance outweighed the added bias. Therefore, a low MSE alone does not show that a design supports valid inference.

Figure~\ref{fig:alloc-dist} shows how MSE varied across individual assignments. Under control-only spillover, Incidence-Guided Saturation's worst-decile mean MSE on the grid was 0.191 against 0.346 for SRS, and under both-arms spillover it was 0.058 against 0.054. Outcome noise inflated this spread, accounting for a median 63\% of the variance in Incidence-Guided Saturation's estimated risk under control-only spillover (SI Section 12.3).

\subsection{SUD case study: Incidence-Guided Saturation had lower error than SRS under control-only spillover}
Every case-study setting, including the sensitivity analyses, completed without estimation failures or non-identified coefficients and met the precision targets.

Under control-only spillover, Incidence-Guided Saturation's mean MSE was 21.1--29.9\% lower than the SRS value of 0.308, depending on the year (Figure~\ref{fig:nc-mse}, with absolute values in SI Table S17). It had lower MSE than SRS in every primary parameter setting, and each setting's difference was more than 1.96 MCSEs from zero. The control-only MSE of High Incidence Focus ranged from 0.70 to 1.40 times that of SRS across years, because the clusters it treats change with each year's incidence (Figure~\ref{fig:nc-map}C). With both-arms spillover, SRS mean MSE was 0.076 and that of Incidence-Guided Saturation ranged from 0.074 to 0.077 across years, small differences that do not show either design to be better. Across the six designs and SRS and both regimes, averaged over years, mean coverage of the $t$-based interval was 94.2--94.9\%, compared with 92.7--93.6\% for Wald intervals.

\CTJMeanFigure
\fi

On the NC map the best design again depended on the regime, but several grid results changed. Checkerboard's effect was identifiable, but it had roughly three times SRS's MSE in both regimes. Isolation Buffer was close to SRS under control-only spillover but had 3.74 times its MSE under both-arms spillover. Spatial blocking was close to SRS under both-arms spillover but varied by year under control-only spillover. Therefore, a design's ranking on the grid need not carry over unchanged to an irregular geography.

In the case study under control-only spillover at $\rho\in\{0,0.5\}$ and $\psi=0.8$, Incidence-Guided Saturation's worst-decile mean was 20.7--43.4\% below that of SRS across the eight year-by-$\rho$ settings (Figure~\ref{fig:alloc-dist}). The pattern held when one half of the outcomes selected the worst decile and the other half evaluated it. However, each worst decile held only ten assignments. Under both-arms spillover, most of the variance in Incidence-Guided Saturation's estimated risk was Monte Carlo error (SI Section 14). Therefore, small both-arms differences do not show better downside protection.

With both-arms spillover, every design except Checkerboard and Isolation Buffer needed 35--37 colleges for 80\% power at $\beta=1$, one residual standard deviation. Under control-only spillover, no design reached 80\% power at that effect with all 58 colleges, and Incidence-Guided Saturation's MSE advantage over SRS in this setting fell from 22\% with 58 colleges to 5\% with 29.

High Incidence Focus treated 29 clusters (SI Table S5) but reached 22--24\% of the working-age population, while Isolation Buffer, with about 16 treated clusters, reached about 27\%.

Letting incidence shift the simulated outcome ($\tau=1$) and adding incidence as a covariate in the fitted model increased High Incidence Focus MSE roughly fourfold under both-arms spillover and up to 2.5-fold under control-only spillover. Aligning treatment with incidence leaves little information about $\beta$ after adjustment. The two changes were made together. Therefore, their separate contributions cannot be distinguished. Rook and queen contiguity at matched parameter values led to the same broad conclusions.

\section{Discussion}
\subsection{Implications for trial design}
The grid study and observed-incidence case study support considering saturation when spillover mainly reaches controls and most colleges take part, and retaining SRS among competitive options when both arms are exposed. Average MSE and allocation risk should be evaluated together. A restricted design with little variation across assignments is not attractive if its error is high for every assignment, and small, noisy differences among the worst assignments are not enough to dismiss SRS. Isolation Buffer's protection against poor control-only assignments (Figure~\ref{fig:alloc-dist}) is useful only when both-arms spillover is implausible and treating fewer clusters is acceptable.

Incidence-Guided Saturation was not best in every setting. Under both-arms spillover its MSE was 8\% above that of SRS on the grid. Under control-only spillover its advantage narrowed as $\beta$ grew, and at $\beta=3$ Isolation Buffer had lower MSE.

The low both-arms error of High Incidence Focus in the case study (Figure~\ref{fig:nc-mse}) came from a fixed assignment and was sensitive to incidence adjustment. Therefore, it does not support a general recommendation. Which regime applies depends on how an intervention spreads between neighboring areas, and investigators need to judge which is more plausible when planning. Saturation keeps both arms in every region while varying the local share treated, which helps separate the direct effect from neighboring exposure.

\subsection{Limitations}
A good allocation does not remove the need for an analysis that models spillover. The primary model used the true form of spillover. We did not assess performance when the assumed neighbor network is wrong, when treatment effects vary between clusters, or when outcomes are measured on samples of individuals within clusters. The grid and one irregular geography do not cover all trial settings. With different numbers of clusters treated by each design, the rankings are not comparisons at equal cost. Historical incidence was treated as fixed, so we did not account for its measurement error or for changes between planning and implementation. Worst-decile estimates also depend on the sampled assignments and the finite number of outcomes per assignment.

\subsection{Continued work for a statewide extension of the SUD education pilot}
A practical choice should weigh recruitment and exposure consequences alongside MSE. Before using these results to choose an allocation for the statewide extension, investigators would need to define the education endpoint, identify eligible healthcare professionals, specify the staff and other resources available to deliver the education, and set cluster and participant budgets. The expected effect size and outcome variability must be established first, because power was low under control-only spillover. Investigators should also assess whether geographic contiguity reflects how professionals actually communicate. These choices could change outcome precision or the exposure network.

\subsection{Conclusions}
The education case study is a design evaluation, not an implemented intervention or evidence that education reduces SUD. Within the assumptions evaluated, Incidence-Guided Saturation had the lowest or near-lowest average error and lower worst-decile error than SRS under control-only spillover, while giving the highest-burden region the largest treated share. SRS remains a reasonable choice when spillover reaches both arms.
