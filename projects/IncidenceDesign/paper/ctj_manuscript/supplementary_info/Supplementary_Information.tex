\section{Simulation setting}\label{detailed-simulation-methods}



\subsection{Spatial structure and outcome model}\label{spatial-structure-and-outcome-model}


We simulated a \(10\times10\) regular grid of 100 clusters. Under queen
contiguity, two clusters are neighbors if they share an edge or a corner.
Under rook contiguity, they must share an edge. Queen contiguity is
primary. Rook contiguity is a sensitivity analysis and matches the
neighbors used on the grids of the earlier small-grid study. For
each definition, the binary adjacency matrix was row-standardized to
form the spatial weights matrix \(\mathbf{W}\), so that \(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{Z}\) gives, for each
cluster, the proportion of its neighbors that are treated, and \(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{Y}\)
gives the average outcome among its neighbors. Row-standardization makes each cluster's spillover exposure an average over its own neighborhood, which keeps exposure comparable between clusters with different numbers of neighbors. Within a scenario, the same neighbor
type defines \(\mathbf{W}\) in the outcome model, in the Isolation Buffer design,
and in the fitted model.

Cluster-level outcomes \(\mathbf{Y}\) were generated from a spatial lag model
with a spillover term \citep{ord_estimation_1975,lesage_introduction_2009}, \begin{equation}
\mathbf{Y} = (\mathbf{I} - \rho \mathbf{W})^{-1}\left[\beta \mathbf{Z} + \psi\,\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{Z}) + \tau \mathbf{X} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\right], \qquad \boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \sim N(\mathbf{0}, \sigma^2 \mathbf{I}),
\label{eq:outcome}
\end{equation} where \(\mathbf{Z} \in \{0,1\}^{100}\) is the treatment assignment
produced by the design under evaluation, \(\beta\) is the direct
treatment coefficient before spatial propagation, \(\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{Z})\) is the spillover term defined in
Equation \ref{eq:spillover}, \(\rho\) is the spatial autoregressive
coefficient governing dependence in the outcome beyond that induced by
treatment and spillover, \(\mathbf{X}\) is the baseline incidence covariate with
coefficient \(\tau = 1\), and \(\sigma = 1\). Section~\ref{incidence-coefficient} explains how \(\tau\) can be estimated after a trial. The spatial multiplier
\((\mathbf{I}-\rho \mathbf{W})^{-1}\) propagates each cluster's shocks through the network
of neighbors. We tested \(\rho \in \{0, 0.01, 0.20, 0.50\}\). When
\(\rho = 0\) the multiplier is the identity and the model is a linear
regression with independent errors,
\(\mathbf{Y} = \beta \mathbf{Z} + \psi\,\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{Z}) + \tau \mathbf{X} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\), in which spatial structure
enters only through the spillover term. Larger values of \(\rho\) induce
progressively stronger spatial clustering in the outcome.

We evaluated two spillover regimes, \begin{equation}
\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{Z}) = \mathbf{W}\mathbf{Z} \quad \text{(both arms)}, \qquad \mathbf{S}(\mathbf{Z}) = (\mathbf{1} - \mathbf{Z}) \circ \mathbf{W}\mathbf{Z} \quad \text{(control only)},
\label{eq:spillover}
\end{equation} where \(\circ\) multiplies the two vectors cluster by cluster. The share of treated neighbors \(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{Z}\) maps the full assignment to each cluster's exposure \citep{aronow_estimating_2017,forastiere_identification_2021}.
Control-only spillover represents classical contamination. The exposure
term enters the outcome equation only for untreated clusters. An
untreated cluster's outcome rises with the share of its neighbors that
are treated. The spatial multiplier can still propagate that contribution
to treated clusters. Under both-arms spillover, the exposure term
enters the equation for treated and untreated clusters. Neighboring
treatment then adds to a treated cluster's own effect. We tested four spillover magnitudes,
\(\psi \in \{0.5, 0.6, 0.7, 0.8\}\), and five magnitudes of the direct
effect, \(\beta \in \{0.8, 1.0, 1.5, 2.0, 3.0\}\), with \(\beta = 1\) as
the primary scenario throughout.


\subsection{Heterogeneous baseline incidence}\label{heterogeneous-baseline-incidence}


The earlier small-grid study \citep{walther_spatialcrt_2026} assumed a
spatially uniform baseline outcome risk. Cluster-level health outcomes
typically vary across space, and the designs studied here differ in
whether and how they use that variation. We generated the baseline
incidence covariate \(\mathbf{X}\), with values in \((0, 1)\), under three
mechanisms. Under the independent (iid) mechanism, each \(X_i\) is drawn
from a Uniform\((0,1)\) distribution, with no spatial structure. Under
the spatially autocorrelated mechanism,
\(\mathbf{X} = \Phi\{(\mathbf{I} - \rho_X \mathbf{W}_Q)^{-1} \mathbf{u}\}\), where \(\mathbf{u} \sim N(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})\),
\(\Phi\) is the standard normal distribution function applied
elementwise, \(\mathbf{W}_Q\) is the queen weights matrix, and
\(\rho_X \in \{0.20, 0.50\}\). Under the Poisson mechanism, a spatially
autocorrelated log-rate surface,
\(\log \boldsymbol{\lambda} = \log(35/100{,}000) + (\mathbf{I} - \rho_X \mathbf{W}_Q)^{-1} \mathbf{u}\) with
\(\rho_X \in \{0.20, 0.50\}\), generates cluster death counts
\(C_i \sim \text{Poisson}(\lambda_i P)\), with an equal population of
\(P = 100{,}000\) in every cluster, so that a cluster at the base rate
expects 35 deaths. The observed rates \(C_i / P\) are then
rank-normalized to \(X_i = (r_i - 0.5)/N\), where \(r_i\) is the rank of
cluster \(i\)'s rate among the \(N = 100\) clusters and tied rates share
their average rank. Ties remain at this population size, and each
Poisson surface has only 60 to 76 distinct values among its 100
clusters. We set the base rate at 35 per 100,000, near the average of
the sudden unexpected death (SUD) rates that Mirzaei and colleagues
estimated for US men (49.3 per 100,000) and women (21.7 per 100,000)
aged 20--64 \citep{mirzaei_years_2019}. It is not the observed rate in
the case study, whose data come from a different source, age range,
period, and case definition. The incidence
filters always use the queen weights \(\mathbf{W}_Q\), whichever neighbor type
the scenario uses, so the incidence surfaces are identical under rook
and queen contiguity. The iid mechanism and the two strengths of each of
the other two mechanisms give five incidence configurations.

For each configuration we generated \(K = 10\) incidence surfaces,
\(\mathbf{X}_1, \dots, \mathbf{X}_{10}\), each from its own seed. The same ten surfaces
are used by every scenario in that configuration, across neighbor types,
outcome-model parameters, designs, and values of \(\beta\). Section
\ref{simulation-design-resampling-and-metrics} describes how the design
draws and the outcome noise are shared. Each design is applied
to the surface that generates the outcome: assignments for surface \(k\)
are drawn from \(\mathbf{X}_k\), and the outcome and the fitted model for those
assignments use \(\mathbf{X}_k\) as well. This assumes that the incidence
observed when a trial is planned is the incidence that drives its
outcome (the matched-surface assumption). The target of each scenario is
performance averaged over incidence surfaces. For example, the mean squared error (MSE) is \(\text{MSE} = E_{\mathbf{X}} E_{\mathbf{Z} \mid \mathbf{X}} E_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}[(\hat\beta - \beta)^2]\), with
\(\mathbf{X}\) drawn from the configuration's generator, \(\mathbf{Z}\) from the design
given \(\mathbf{X}\), and \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \sim N(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})\). Bias, coverage, and power
are defined in the same way.

\section{Treatment assignment designs}\label{treatment-assignment-designs}


We implemented eight treatment assignment designs. Six form the
comparison set of the detailed analysis and are described here. The other
two, Saturation Quadrants and Balanced Halves, are variants of retained
designs. Section \ref{app:variants} defines them and explains why they
were left out of the detailed ranking.

\iffullsi Table \ref{tab:designs} describes the six retained designs. Figure \ref{fig:design-samples}\else
Table~\ref{M-tab:allocation-rules} of the article describes the six retained designs and\fi\ shows one assignment
from each design and one from simple random sampling (SRS). The examples
use a separate spatially autocorrelated incidence surface with
$\rho_X = 0.5$, generated with a fixed seed.

Checkerboard is the strict Block Stratified Sampling allocation of the
earlier small-grid study, in which no two rook neighbors share an arm.
That design allowed relaxed allocations on its \(3\times3\) grid because
the arms could not be balanced. On its \(2\times4\) and \(3\times4\)
grids, as on the \(10\times10\) grid here, the strict allocations are the
two checkerboard patterns. We call this allocation Checkerboard hereafter.

Checkerboard is the only deterministic design. Its assignment is fixed,
and every fit gets its own outcome noise. We drew every other design
independently 25 times per incidence surface. Designs that rank clusters
by incidence break ties at random for each draw. High Incidence Focus
varies only through tie-breaking. Under the iid and spatial mechanisms,
incidence values have no ties, and its assignment is fixed within each
surface and varies across surfaces. Isolation Buffer uses the neighbor
type of the scenario and treats an average of 38 clusters with rook
contiguity and 21 with queen contiguity. Every other design treats exactly 50.

\subsection{The two related variants}\label{app:variants}


Table \ref{tab:excluded-designs} defines Saturation Quadrants and
Balanced Halves\iffullsi\ in the format of Table \ref{tab:designs}\fi. Each shares the
mechanism of a retained design, and in the comparison of the eight designs
and SRS at \(\beta = 1\), neither could be
distinguished from its counterpart under either neighbor type by the
Nemenyi and Holm-adjusted Wilcoxon signed-rank tests described in Section
\ref{statistical-comparison-methods}. We kept one design from each pair
for the detailed ranking. Both variants remain in the case study, and Section
\ref{app:eight-design} reports the comparison of all eight designs and SRS at the
primary scenario, \(\beta = 1\).

Saturation Quadrants gives the quadrants their saturations in random
order, whereas Incidence-Guided Saturation gives the higher saturations
to the quadrants with higher mean incidence. Saturation Quadrants' mean
squared error was 0.090, against 0.091 for Incidence-Guided Saturation,
under queen contiguity (Nemenyi \(p = 0.99\); Holm-adjusted Wilcoxon
signed-rank \(p = 0.62\)), and 0.071 against 0.072 under rook
contiguity (Nemenyi \(p = 1.00\); Wilcoxon \(p = 1.00\)). It also had
the better average rank among the eight designs and SRS (2.394 against 2.700 under queen contiguity and
2.587 against 2.650 under rook contiguity), so keeping Incidence-Guided
Saturation does not rest on performance. Incidence guidance matches the
accuracy of a random quadrant order while directing the highest
saturations to the quadrants or regions with the highest incidence. How
much treatment it redirects depends on how much incidence differs between
regions (Figure \ref{fig:spread-gain}). The accuracy advantage of both
designs over SRS, which arises under control-only spillover (Section
\ref{srs-benchmark-and-allocation-specific-risk}), comes from the
saturation structure they share. On the North Carolina map, both designs use four
fixed geographic regions in place of the quadrants.

Balanced Halves divides clusters into two halves by incidence rank and
treats 25 at random in each. Its mean squared error was 0.130, against
0.131 for Balanced Quartiles, under queen contiguity (Nemenyi
\(p = 1.00\); Wilcoxon \(p = 1.00\)), and 0.084 against 0.084 under
rook contiguity (Nemenyi \(p = 1.00\); Wilcoxon \(p = 1.00\)). The two
designs differ only in how finely they stratify on incidence, and we kept
the finer, four-stratum version. Balanced Halves had the marginally lower
mean and the better average rank under queen contiguity (3.831 against
3.906), and Balanced Quartiles had both under rook contiguity (3.369
against 3.569), so this choice does not rest on performance either.

\subsection{Variants of incidence guidance}\label{igs-variants-methods}

Incidence-Guided Saturation fixes two choices, four quadrants and saturations from 80\% down to 20\%. To see how much each matters, we varied the number of regions and the spread of the saturations on the grid. The grid was divided into 2 halves, 4 quadrants, 10 rectangles or 25 blocks of equal size. With \(R\) regions and spread \(s\), the region ranked \(r\) by mean incidence received the target saturation \(0.5 + s\{1 - 2(r-1)/(R-1)\}\), so the highest-ranked region was treated at \(50\% + s\) and the lowest at \(50\% - s\). We used \(s = 0.1\), 0.2, 0.3, 0.4 and 0.5, which give highest and lowest saturations of 60/40, 70/30, 80/20, 90/10 and 100/0 percent. Four quadrants at 80/20 is the original design.

The 100/0 setting is a boundary case, included only to show whether going beyond 90/10 helps or hurts. It treats one region fully and leaves another untreated. For quadrants its targets are 100\%, 67\%, 33\% and 0\%, or 25, 17, 8 and 0 treated clusters. Targets were rounded by largest remainders so that every assignment treated exactly 50 of the 100 clusters, and ties in regional mean incidence were broken at random. Each guided variant has an unguided twin that gives the same targets to the regions in random order, so the pair isolates the effect of incidence guidance. The unguided twin of the original design is Saturation Quadrants. With 25 blocks at 60/40, every block has two treated clusters, so both versions are \(2\times2\) Blocking and the pair serves as a null control.

The 38 new variants, the original design, Saturation Quadrants and SRS were run on the full grid of Section~\ref{simulation-design-resampling-and-metrics}, with the same incidence surfaces and outcome noise as the other designs, 25 assignments per surface and both fitted models. We report queen contiguity and the oracle model, pooled over the five incidence configurations and the five values of \(\beta\) within each spillover regime. Each comparison uses the 50 paired configuration-by-surface differences in MSE, with a two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test and Holm adjustment within each family. The families compare each design with SRS (40 comparisons), each guided variant with the original design (19) and each guided variant with its twin (20). For the figure, the paired 95\% \(t_{49}\) interval for the mean difference is divided by the comparator's MSE and shifted by 1, which puts it on the ratio scale. Every 100/0 assignment gave full-rank design matrices, and no fit of a new variant was aliased or failed.

\section{Estimation and identification}\label{estimation}


We fit two maximum likelihood spatial lag models with intercepts to each
simulated outcome. The primary, oracle model includes the spatial lag
\(\rho \mathbf{W}\mathbf{Y}\) and the covariates \(\mathbf{Z}\),
\(\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{Z})\) and \(\mathbf{X}_k\). It matches the outcome
model in Equation \ref{eq:outcome}, using the true regime-specific
spillover term in Equation \ref{eq:spillover}. The oracle model assumes
that the analyst knows the form of spillover and has the exact exposure
term, so its results are a best case for each design. The non-oracle
model, a sensitivity analysis, includes \(\mathbf{Z}\) and
\(\mathbf{X}_k\) and omits the spillover term.

We fit both models with our own implementation of the maximum likelihood
estimator of the spatial lag model \citep{bivand_comparing_2015}, the
same estimator that the \texttt{lagsarlm} function of the R package
\texttt{spatialreg} computes with \texttt{method\ =\ "eigen"}. It
maximizes the concentrated log-likelihood over \(\rho\), computing the
log-determinant from the eigenvalues of \(\mathbf{W}\)
\citep{ord_estimation_1975}, estimates the remaining coefficients by
least squares given \(\hat\rho\), and computes standard errors from the
analytic information matrix. On 5,120 validation fits it agreed with
\texttt{lagsarlm} to within \(9.7\times10^{-8}\) in \(\hat\beta\) and to
a relative difference of \(1.1\times10^{-8}\) in its standard error, at
roughly 1\% of the computing time. We also refit a 1\% sample of the
full simulation with \texttt{lagsarlm} itself as a check. Confidence intervals are 95\% intervals with a $t$ reference distribution and a degrees-of-freedom-corrected standard error, $\hat\beta \pm t_{0.975,\,N-p}\,\widehat{\text{SE}}\sqrt{N/(N-p)}$. Here $\widehat{\text{SE}}$ is the estimated standard error, $N = 100$ is the number of clusters, and $p$ is the number of regression coefficients plus $\rho$. The oracle model has $p = 5$, and the non-oracle model and rook Checkerboard, whose aliased spillover column is dropped, have $p = 4$. The case study uses the same interval. The 95\% Wald interval, $\hat\beta \pm 1.96\,\widehat{\text{SE}}$, covered about one percentage point less for every design.

We retain Checkerboard in the design comparison and flag aliased estimates.
Section \ref{identification-diagnostics} explains why rook Checkerboard
cannot separate treatment from spillover and why queen Checkerboard
estimates the treatment effect imprecisely under control-only spillover.
It also describes the rare Isolation Buffer aliases.


\subsection{Identification diagnostics}\label{identification-diagnostics}


Two combinations of design and neighbor type weaken or remove the
information that separates \(\beta\) from the spillover term. Under rook
contiguity, Checkerboard gives \(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{Z} = \mathbf{1} - \mathbf{Z}\) exactly, because every
neighbor of a treated cluster is a control and every neighbor of a
control is treated. Both spillover covariates then equal
\(\mathbf{1} - \mathbf{Z}\), a linear combination of the intercept and \(\mathbf{Z}\), so
\(\beta\) is not identifiable. The fitting routine drops the aliased
spillover column, and the coefficient on \(\mathbf{Z}\) estimates
$\beta - \psi$, because $\psi(\mathbf{1} - \mathbf{Z}) = \psi - \psi\mathbf{Z}$. The earlier small-grid study noted the same
consequence of exact collinearity between treatment and spillover
exposure. The non-oracle model has no spillover column to drop, but its Checkerboard
estimates under rook contiguity have the same defect. Under queen
contiguity, each of the 64 interior clusters has four treated and four
control neighbors, so \((\mathbf{W}\mathbf{Z})_i = 1/2\) at every interior cluster. Under the
control-only regime the spillover covariate then equals
\((1/2)(\mathbf{1} - \mathbf{Z})\) at those clusters, a linear combination of the
intercept and \(\mathbf{Z}\), and only the 36 clusters on the grid boundary carry
information that separates it from the intercept and \(\mathbf{Z}\).
Therefore, \(\beta\) is identifiable but imprecisely estimated. Under the both-arms regime it is the
constant \(1/2\) at interior clusters, confounded with the
intercept but not with \(\mathbf{Z}\). Isolation Buffer under rook contiguity can
also, rarely, draw exactly the checkerboard pattern, a maximal
independent set that equals one color class of the grid. This happened
in 4 of its 40,000 rook draws and affected one fit in each of 20
scenarios, 4 at each value of \(\beta\). We flag every fit in which the
fitting routine reports an aliased column and every scenario in which
some draw has \(\text{rank}([\mathbf{1}, \mathbf{Z}, \mathbf{W}\mathbf{Z}]) < 3\). Aliased estimates are
kept in all summaries. In the exhibits, we flag Checkerboard under rook
contiguity, where every fit is aliased. The Isolation Buffer scenarios,
each with one aliased fit among its 250, carry no flag in the exhibits.

\subsection{Estimating the incidence coefficient after a trial}\label{incidence-coefficient}

The incidence coefficient \(\tau\) in Equation~\ref{eq:outcome} gives
the change in the outcome's linear predictor for a one-unit increase in
scaled incidence \(\mathbf{X}\), the full width of its 0-to-1 range,
holding treatment and spillover fixed. The change is in residual
standard deviations because \(\sigma=1\), and is measured before
spatial propagation. At \(\tau=1\), a one-unit incidence contrast
contributes as much to the linear predictor as the primary intervention
effect \(\beta=1\). At \(\tau=0\) incidence makes no direct
contribution to that predictor. The coefficient can exceed 1 or be
negative.

Before outcomes are observed, investigators specify \(\tau\) for the
planning model. After a trial, the same spatial lag model can estimate
\(\tau\) and \(\beta\) from the observed outcomes and the incidence
used for allocation. Both grid models include \(\mathbf{X}_k\)
(Section~\ref{estimation}), and every fit provides both estimates.
The oracle estimate describes the association of prior incidence
with the outcome after adjustment for treatment and spillover. The
non-oracle model adjusts for treatment and omits spillover.
Incidence is not randomized. Therefore, these coefficients describe adjusted associations between incidence and the outcome.

To see how well \(\tau\) can be estimated, and whether its value changes the design comparison, we repeated the full grid benchmark at \(\tau = 0\), 0.25, 0.5, 1 and 2. The incidence surfaces, assignments and outcome noise were the same at every value of \(\tau\), so only the incidence term of the outcome changed. The 95\% interval for \(\tau\) has the same \(t\)-based, degrees-of-freedom-corrected form as the interval for \(\beta\), and power is the proportion of intervals that exclude zero, a two-sided test at level 0.05. At \(\tau = 1\) the rerun reproduced every stored intervention result of the benchmark exactly.

With the oracle model, the largest absolute bias of \(\hat\tau\) across designs, incidence configurations, neighbor types, regimes and values of \(\tau\) was 0.023. The intervals covered \(\tau\) in 93.8--95.8\% of fits, and at \(\tau = 0\) they excluded zero in 4.3--6.2\% of fits. Without the spillover term, bias reached 0.137, because omitting spillover can shift the fitted association with incidence.

The design affected the precision of \(\hat\tau\) far more than the value of \(\tau\) did. On the queen grid with independent uniform incidence, the mean model standard error of \(\hat\tau\) was 0.667--0.670 for High Incidence Focus and 0.336--0.337 for SRS, and every other design was between 0.334 and 0.341. At \(\tau = 1\), the test rejected in 29.3--30.3\% of High Incidence Focus trials and in 81.1--81.9\% of SRS trials across the two spillover regimes (Figure~\ref{fig:incidence-rejection}). High Incidence Focus reached that level of power only at \(\tau = 2\), where it rejected in 81.3--81.5\% of trials. It had the largest standard error of any design in every incidence configuration, neighbor type and regime. High Incidence Focus treats the clusters above the median incidence, so its mean absolute correlation between \(\mathbf{Z}\) and \(\mathbf{X}\) was 0.866, against 0.079--0.080 for SRS, and little variation in \(\mathbf{X}\) remains once treatment is in the model. For uniform \(\mathbf{X}\), splitting treatment at the median leaves one quarter of the variance of \(\mathbf{X}\) within the arms. In a simple regression with residual standard deviation 1, this doubles the standard error of the incidence coefficient from \(\sqrt{12/100} = 0.35\) to 0.69. Incidence-Guided Saturation also uses incidence, but by region and with every region partly treated, so its correlation was 0.109--0.110 and its standard error 0.339--0.341, close to that of SRS.

Changing \(\tau\) barely moved \(\hat\beta\). Over the five values of \(\tau\), no design's mean MSE for \(\beta\) changed by more than 3.4\% from its value at \(\tau = 1\) in any incidence configuration, neighbor type or regime. Both fitted models include \(\mathbf{X}\). Changing \(\tau\) leaves the fitted intervention coefficient unchanged when \(\rho\) is held at its true value. Error in estimating \(\rho\) can pass part of the change into \(\hat\beta\) (Section~\ref{app:technical-notes}). The spillover regime still decided which designs led. Under control-only spillover, Incidence-Guided Saturation or Saturation Quadrants had the lowest MSE in every comparison. Under both-arms spillover, the best design's MSE was at most 5\% below that of SRS. The lowest-MSE design changed with \(\tau\) in 6 of the 20 comparisons, and in 5 of these the two saturation designs, whose MSEs were nearly equal, exchanged first place.

The oracle results assume the correct form of spillover, and the non-oracle bias shows that \(\hat\tau\) depends on it. The grid used one number of clusters, ten incidence surfaces per configuration and nonnegative values of \(\tau\), and \(\hat\tau\) is on the scale of incidence after it was scaled to the unit interval. The primary case-study model omits incidence, because the education outcome is not expected to depend on it, so these results give no estimate of \(\tau\) for that trial.

\section{Simulation design, resampling and metrics}\label{simulation-design-resampling-and-metrics}


The full simulation crossed five values of \(\beta\), five incidence
configurations, two neighbor types, four values of \(\rho\), four values
of \(\psi\), two spillover regimes, and eight designs, for
\(5 \times 5 \times 2 \times 4 \times 4 \times 2 \times 8 = 12{,}800\)
scenarios, of which the six designs reported in the detailed analysis
account for 9,600. We call a combination of incidence configuration,
neighbor type, \(\rho\), \(\psi\), and spillover regime a block. There
are \(5 \times 2 \times 4 \times 4 \times 2 = 320\) blocks, 160 for each
neighbor type. At the primary scenario, \(\beta = 1\), the six designs
give 1,920 scenarios, and every block contains all six.

We drew \(J = 25\) assignments for each of the \(K = 10\) incidence
surfaces, for 250 fits per scenario, with a separate noise vector for
each fit. Fit \((k,j)\) uses outcome
\(\mathbf{Y}_{kj} = (\mathbf{I} - \rho \mathbf{W})^{-1}[\beta \mathbf{Z}_{kj} + \psi\,\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{Z}_{kj}) + \tau \mathbf{X}_k + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{kj}]\).
The assignment is fixed for all 250 Checkerboard fits. Within each
surface the 25 pairs of assignments and noise vectors are independent.
The ten surfaces are also independent, making the surface the primary
sampling unit. Each model was fit 3.2 million times for the eight
designs. SRS randomly treats 50 of the 100 clusters with no restrictions
on incidence or geography. We ran it afterward using the same surfaces,
noise and 250 fits per scenario. This brought the benchmark to 14,400
scenarios and 3.6 million fits per model and left the eight-design
results unchanged.

We seeded each draw by hashing its identifiers. A surface uses its
configuration and index. A set of 25 assignments uses its block,
surface and design. The outcome noise uses its block. This makes the
draws reproducible in any run order, and the shared keys give common
random numbers. Within each block, all designs share the same surfaces
and noise, the five values of \(\beta\) share the same assignments and
noise, and both models use the same outcomes. Assignments and noise are
independent across blocks. Blocks in the same incidence configuration
share its ten surfaces.

For each scenario and estimator, we computed five metrics over the valid
fits, those with a finite estimate and standard error: bias, the mean of
\(\hat\beta - \beta\); the standard deviation (SD) of \(\hat\beta\); mean
squared error, \(\text{MSE} = \text{mean}\{(\hat\beta - \beta)^2\}\);
coverage, the fraction of fits with \(L \le \beta \le U\), where \(L\) and \(U\) are the limits of the 95\% interval defined in Section \ref{estimation}; and power, the
fraction of fits with \(L > 0\), a one-sided test of \(\beta = 0\) at
level 0.025. For both estimators, every one of the 250 fits in every
scenario was valid, and the fitting routine raised no warning other than
the aliasing flags described above.

We computed Monte Carlo standard errors for bias, MSE, coverage and
power from the surface-level metrics \citep{morris_using_2019}. For a
metric \(u\), we computed \(u_k\) on the 25 fits from surface \(k\) and
set \(\text{SE}(u) = \text{sd}(u_1,\dots,u_{10})/\sqrt{10}\), with
intervals \(u \pm t_{0.975,\,9}\,\text{SE}(u)\). The standard error of
a paired difference uses \(u_{d,k}-u_{d',k}\) over \(k\). For pooled
results we apply the same formula to the 50 independent
configuration-by-surface means. When pooling both regimes, each mean
averages that surface's 32 blocks. The pooled standard deviation
includes variation across configurations. \iffullsi Section
\ref{app:mechanics} gives the parameter grid, the seed keys, and the
exact formulas for these metrics and standard errors.\else Table \ref{tab:parameter-grid} gives the parameter grid.\fi


\subsection{Statistical comparisons and reporting}\label{statistical-comparison-methods}


We compared designs within each neighbor type with a Friedman rank-sum
test on scenario MSE \citep{friedman_use_1937}, treating the 160 blocks
as repeated measures across the six designs and SRS. SRS is the usual default for a cluster randomized trial, so it enters every test as a seventh member and each design is compared with it as well as with the other designs. At \(\beta = 1\) we followed
the Friedman test with Nemenyi post-hoc comparisons
\citep{nemenyi_distributionfree_1963, demsar_statistical_2006} and
paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests \citep{wilcoxon_individual_1945} with
Holm's adjustment for multiple comparisons \citep{holm_simple_1979} for
all 21 pairs, and we repeated the Friedman test at each other
value of \(\beta\). Blocks within an incidence configuration share their
surfaces, so these tests treat dependent blocks as independent, which
overstates the information about variation from surface to surface. Therefore, we also ran paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests over 50 independent units, the 5
configurations times 10 surfaces, with each unit's MSE averaged over
that surface's blocks, and approximate \(t_{49}\) intervals for the mean
difference. These tests compared each pair adjacent in the mean-MSE order, with SRS in its place in that order, and each design with SRS within each spillover regime, with Holm's adjustment over the eight designs compared with SRS.

We report results separately for each neighbor type, with queen
contiguity primary, and no number is averaged over the two. Unless
stated otherwise, every number in the Supplementary Results is pooled over the five
incidence configurations, and every table and figure caption states
whether it pools the configurations or shows them separately. We report
the ranking within each configuration separately and show the split
wherever a conclusion changes.


\subsection{Allocation-risk study}\label{allocation-risk-study-design}


A separate allocation-risk study used queen weights, \(\beta=1\), all ten surfaces from each incidence configuration and the extreme combinations of $\rho$ and $\psi$, $\rho\in\{0,0.5\}$ and $\psi\in\{0.5,0.8\}$. The risk of assignment $a$ is its conditional MSE $m(a)=E_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}[(\hat\beta-\beta)^2\mid a,\mathbf{X}]$. We estimated it by simulating repeated outcomes for each sampled assignment and then compared its distribution across assignments. This distribution differs from that
of squared errors in a single allocation and from MSE across parameter
scenarios. The study used 100 assignment draws for each randomized design and 100 simulated outcomes per distinct assignment. One setting (surface 1, $\rho=0.5$, $\psi=0.8$) was refined to 400 outcomes on the same assignments as a precision check. A design with only one possible assignment received its own outcome replication.

\sihead{Monte Carlo structure and checkpoints}
Within a scenario, fit
\((k, j)\), with \(k = 1, \dots, 10\) and \(j = 1, \dots, 25\), uses
surface \(\mathbf{X}_k\), assignment \(\mathbf{Z}_{kj}\), and noise vector
\(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{kj}\), the \((k, j)\) column of the block's
\(100 \times 250\) noise matrix (Section \ref{simulation-design-resampling-and-metrics}). The unit of computation is a combination of incidence
configuration, neighbor type, and \(\rho\): 40 units, each covering 4
values of \(\psi\), 2 spillover regimes, 8 designs, 5 values of
\(\beta\), 250 fits, and 2 estimators. The units ran in parallel, and the
run stops if any unit fails. Each unit's results are saved together with
a record of the parameter settings, the simulation code, and the
software versions that produced them, and saved results are reused only
if all of these match the current run, so results from different code or
settings are never combined.

\sihead{Scenario metrics}
For one scenario and estimator, let
\(f = 1, \dots, n\) index the valid fits, those with a finite estimate
and standard error (here every fit was valid, so \(n = 250\)). Let
\(L_f, U_f = \hat\beta_f \mp t_{0.975,\,N-p}\,\widehat{\text{SE}}_f\sqrt{N/(N-p)}\), with \(N = 100\) clusters and \(p\) as defined in Section \ref{estimation} (the Wald limits replace the multiplier by \(z_{0.975} = \Phi^{-1}(0.975) \approx 1.96\)), and let
\(\bar{\hat\beta} = n^{-1}\sum_f \hat\beta_f\). Then \[
\text{Bias} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{f=1}^{n}(\hat\beta_f - \beta), \qquad
\text{SD} = \left\{\frac{1}{n-1}\sum_{f=1}^{n}\left(\hat\beta_f - \bar{\hat\beta}\right)^2\right\}^{1/2}, \qquad
\text{MSE} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{f=1}^{n}(\hat\beta_f - \beta)^2,
\] \[
\text{Coverage} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{f=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\{L_f \le \beta \le U_f\}, \qquad
\text{Power} = \frac{1}{n}\sum_{f=1}^{n}\mathbb{I}\{L_f > 0\},
\] so that \(\text{MSE} = \text{Bias}^2 + (n-1)\,\text{SD}^2/n\).

\sihead{Surface-level standard errors}
For
\(u \in \{\text{Bias}, \text{MSE}, \text{Coverage}, \text{Power}\}\),
let \(u_k\) be the same formula applied to the valid fits from surface
\(k\) (here all 25). With \(K = 10\) and
\(s(a_1, \dots, a_K) = \{(K-1)^{-1}\sum_k (a_k - \bar a)^2\}^{1/2}\) the
sample standard deviation, \[
\text{SE}(u) = \frac{s(u_1, \dots, u_K)}{\sqrt{K}}, \qquad u \pm t_{0.975,\,K-1}\,\text{SE}(u).
\] Because each surface contributes 25 fits,
\(u = K^{-1}\sum_k u_k\). For two designs \(d\) and \(d'\) in the same
block at the same \(\beta\), the difference is paired at the surface
level: with \(\Delta_k = u_{d,k} - u_{d',k}\), \[
\text{SE}(u_d - u_{d'}) = \frac{s(\Delta_1, \dots, \Delta_K)}{\sqrt{K}},
\] with the same \(t_{0.975,\,K-1}\) interval.

\sihead{Standard errors pooled over configurations}
For one design,
neighbor type, and value of \(\beta\), let \(\mathcal{B}_c\) be the 32
blocks of configuration \(c\) and \(u_{b,k}\) the surface-\(k\) metric
in block \(b\). The configuration-by-surface means and the pooled metric
are \[
\bar u_{c,k} = \frac{1}{32}\sum_{b \in \mathcal{B}_c} u_{b,k}, \qquad
\bar u = \frac{1}{50}\sum_{c=1}^{5}\sum_{k=1}^{10}\bar u_{c,k},
\] where \(\bar u\) equals the mean of the metric over the 160
scenarios, and \[
\text{SE}(\bar u) = \frac{s(\{\bar u_{c,k}\})}{\sqrt{50}},
\] with \(s\) taken over the 50 values \(\bar u_{c,k}\). These are the
standard errors in Table \ref{tab:performance}. For two designs \(d\)
and \(d'\) adjacent in the mean-MSE order (Section \ref{formal-statistical-comparison}), let \(D_{c,k} = \bar u_{d,c,k} - \bar u_{d',c,k}\) for
\(u = \text{MSE}\). The interval for the mean difference is
\(\bar D \pm t_{0.975,\,49}\, s(\{D_{c,k}\})/\sqrt{50}\), and the
Wilcoxon signed-rank test, with the normal approximation, is applied to
the 50 values \(D_{c,k}\).

\section{Observed-incidence case study methods}\label{observed-incidence-application-full-methods-and-results}



\subsection{Purpose and observed inputs}\label{purpose-and-observed-inputs}


The case study asks which allocation designs estimate a known
education-intervention effect accurately on this geography, when some
designs use historical SUD incidence to direct treatment. It informs the
choice of design for a statewide extension of a pilot trial of education
for healthcare professionals involved in SUD prevention. There are no observed intervention outcomes and no
estimated relationship between local SUD incidence and the education
outcome. Any eventual effect on SUD incidence requires a separate
clinical evaluation. The observed inputs are the service-area map, the
county-to-college assignment, and annual deaths and populations. The
outcome model, the effect size, the spatial dependence $\rho$, the
spillover regime and its magnitude $\psi$ are planning assumptions, as
are the volunteering mechanism, the number of professionals per college
and the intraclass correlation in the participation analyses (Section
\ref{partial-participation-methods}). A real trial might realize only one allocation per
planning year, making both average estimation error and poor-allocation
downside relevant.

The assignment units are 58 community college service-area clusters that
partition North Carolina's 100 counties under the study mapping (Figure
\ref{fig:service-areas}). The map uses only county boundaries and the county-to-college assignment. Northampton is assigned to Halifax Community
College, Bertie to Martin Community College, and Hertford alone to
Roanoke-Chowan Community College. We made these assignments for the
analysis only, and they do not limit which counties a college serves. Observed county-year deaths and population estimates for ages 18--64 from the Surveillance, Epidemiology, and End Results program were aggregated to the same named clusters for
each year, 2018--2021. SUD deaths were identified from death
certificates with the case-adjudication algorithm of Gan and colleagues
\citep{gan_factors_2019}. The corrected numerator supplied by Habib
includes heart-failure deaths formerly excluded as presumed non-sudden.
It totals 23,523
deaths over 25,594,321 person-years. The earlier numerator, which
excluded them, reproduces the totals behind Habib's unpublished report
\citep{habib_temporal_2026}. The case study uses the corrected
numerator. We do not infer undocumented upstream exclusion rules or zip-code
allocation from the received county panel.

Annual incidence is \(100,000D_{iy}/P_{iy}\), and pooled incidence is
\(100,000\sum_yD_{iy}/\sum_yP_{iy}\), with the latter population sum
interpreted as person-years. All 58 clusters are present in each year,
and counts and populations sum back to statewide totals. We hold these
observed annual surfaces fixed, generate no synthetic incidence surface
and report years separately (\iffullsi Figure \ref{fig:nc-observed}\else Figure~\ref{M-fig:nc-map}C of the article\fi).

\subsection{Geographic adaptations and treatment budgets}\label{geographic-adaptations-and-treatment-budgets}


Queen contiguity between service areas, computed from legal county
boundaries, defines the primary adjacency, and rook contiguity is a
sensitivity analysis. Before simulation, we checked the 58-cluster
row-standardized contiguity matrix $\mathbf{W}$, its binary adjacency and its row
order against the boundary geometry. Legal boundaries shared across water can make two service areas adjacent. $\mathbf{W}$ is a prespecified geographic proxy for exposure between neighboring service areas.

All eight designs and SRS keep their grid names, adapted to the map as
follows. SRS treats a random 29 of the 58 clusters. High Incidence Focus
treats the 29 clusters with the highest annual rates. Balanced Quartiles
and Balanced Halves stratify clusters by annual rate rank and treat 29
overall, placing at random the extra treatments that odd-size strata
need. In Balanced Halves, one half receives 14 treatments and the other
15, and the half that receives 15 is chosen at random. Isolation Buffer
draws a greedy random maximal independent set on the North Carolina adjacency graph.
Checkerboard becomes a deterministic greedy graph coloring. Clusters are
visited in decreasing order of degree, each receives the arm that
conflicts least with its assigned neighbors, equal conflicts are broken
toward control, and the assignment is complemented if more than half the
clusters are treated. The result keeps Checkerboard's interspersion but
is neither a literal rectangular checkerboard nor an exact bipartite
coloring.

The two saturation designs share one fixed four-region partition in all
years (\iffullsi Figure \ref{fig:nc-regions}\else Figure~\ref{M-fig:nc-map}B of the article\fi). A geographic $k$-means procedure on
standardized projected coordinates generated 250 candidate partitions,
and we selected one by the balance and compactness criterion
\(C=3s_P+1.5s_K+1.5s_D+c/d_{\max}\), without consulting outcomes. Here
\(s_P\), \(s_K\) and \(s_D\) are the standard deviations of the regions'
shares of reference population, clusters and counties, \(c\) averages
within-region distances to the regional centers, and \(d_{\max}\) is the
maximum pairwise distance between the projected cluster coordinates.
Reference population is pooled person-years divided by four. Saturation
Quadrants gives the four regions saturations of 20\%, 40\%, 60\% and
80\% in a random order for each draw. Incidence-Guided Saturation ranks
clusters by annual per-capita rate, averages the ranks within each
region, and gives 80\%, 60\%, 40\% and 20\% to the regions from the
highest mean rank to the lowest, breaking regional ties at random for
each allocation. Treated counts within each region are rounded under the
retained design rules.

Per-capita cluster rates already account for population size, so the
regional mean rank weights clusters equally, whereas a pooled regional
rate (regional deaths divided by regional population) weights larger
clusters more heavily. Mean raw rates and pooled regional rates were prespecified as sensitivity analyses, and Table \ref{tab:nc-order} gives the regional ordering under each
summary, documented before performance was evaluated. The grid rule
averages \(\mathbf{X}\) directly. Mean ranks agree with it in the rank-scaled Poisson configurations and can order regions differently when incidence is continuous. They are a deliberate adaptation for the case study.

On the North Carolina map, $2\times2$ Blocking is the spatial-blocking
adaptation described in Table~\ref{M-tab:allocation-rules} of the article.
The rule uses 14 groups of nearby clusters. Each year, \(k\)-means
on standardized projected coordinates, incidence ranks and
$\log(1+\text{population})$ forms groups of one to eight clusters. We
hold the groups fixed within each year. The rule randomly treats half
of each group, rounded to the nearest whole number, with an exact half
rounded to the even number (1.5 to 2 and 0.5 to 0). Each year has one
single-cluster group, and it stays in control because half of one
cluster rounds to zero. The yearly totals are 28, 31, 27 and 29.

Therefore, treatment budgets (numbers of treated clusters) differ across designs. We kept each design's own rule and report its budget in Table \ref{tab:nc-budgets}.\iffullsi\ Figure \ref{fig:nc-examples} shows one
example assignment from each strategy.\fi

\subsection{Outcome model and Monte Carlo evaluation}\label{outcome-model-and-monte-carlo-evaluation}


For the primary education outcome we set \(\tau=0\) in Equation
\ref{eq:outcome}, use the 58-cluster \(\mathbf{W}\), and fix \(\beta=1\)
and \(\sigma=1\).
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{Y}=(\mathbf{I}-\rho \mathbf{W})^{-1}[\beta \mathbf{Z}+\psi\,\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{Z})+\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}],\qquad \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\sim N(\mathbf{0},\mathbf{I}).
\label{eq:nc-education}
\end{equation}
Observed incidence guides assignment only. We use the same validated
maximum likelihood estimator as on the grid and include an intercept,
\(\mathbf{Z}\) and the true regime-specific \(\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{Z})\),
but not \(\mathbf{X}\). The target is the direct coefficient \(\beta\), the effect of a college's own treatment before outcomes spread through \((\mathbf{I}-\rho \mathbf{W})^{-1}\). Under control-only spillover, a
control college's outcome rises with the share of its neighbors that are
treated. Under both-arms spillover, treated colleges also gain from
treated neighbors. When every neighbor is treated, the spillover term
adds \(\psi=0.5\) or \(0.8\), half or four-fifths of the direct effect.
The case study and the partial-participation analyses use the corrected
$t$ interval in Section \ref{estimation}, with $N$ equal to the number
of participating colleges and $N=58$ in the main case study. For that
interval, $p$ counts the regression coefficients plus $\rho$. We test
$\beta=0$ two-sided at level 0.05 by checking whether the 95\% interval
excludes zero, whereas grid power (Section
\ref{simulation-design-resampling-and-metrics}) is one-sided at level
0.025. In the Wald interval the maximum likelihood variance divides by
$N$, and the normal reference distribution ignores estimation of the
parameters. Both make the Wald interval too narrow with 58 or fewer
colleges.

The primary queen comparison includes 432 reported settings, crossing
\(\rho=0,0.2,0.5\), \(\psi=0.5,0.8\), both regimes, four years,
and the eight designs and SRS. In a matched sensitivity analysis,
we add \(\mathbf{X}=(\operatorname{rank}_{\rm average}(\text{rate})-0.5)/58\)
with \(\tau=1\) to both the outcome model and the fitted covariates
(432 settings). The association it builds in is hypothetical. The rook
sensitivity uses \(\rho=0,0.5\) (288 settings). Two alternative regional
summaries add 96 Incidence-Guided Saturation settings. Settings with the
same possible assignments and outcome model share a simulation, giving
936 distinct simulations for 1,248 reported settings.

We initially sampled $J=100$ assignments and simulated $R=100$
independent outcomes per distinct assignment. A design with one possible
assignment received $R=1,000$ outcomes for that assignment. The
prespecified schedule extended the number of assignments or outcomes
until the Monte Carlo standard error of mean MSE was at most 5\% of mean
MSE and that of coverage was at most 0.01. Two single-assignment settings
in the matched sensitivity analysis required $R=2,000$. All randomized
designs met both targets at $J=100$ and $R=100$. Each random draw used a
fixed seed.

In the primary model, Checkerboard, Saturation Quadrants, Isolation
Buffer and SRS share simulations across years because annual incidence
affects neither their assignments nor their outcomes. We recalculate
treatment counts and population shares for each year. Therefore, their
results in different years are not independent confirmations.

For each allocation $a$, the repeated outcomes estimate the conditional MSE \(m(a)=E_{\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}}[(\hat\beta-\beta)^2\mid a,\text{observed inputs}]\). We report its distribution across sampled allocations, its estimated 90th percentile and the estimated mean among the worst 10\% of sampled allocations, alongside unconditional mean MSE, bias and coverage. An allocation drawn more than once keeps its sampled frequency but uses one conditional estimate, and the Monte Carlo uncertainty accounts for the resulting covariance. A separate tail refinement at queen $\rho=0,0.5$, $\psi=0.8$, both regimes and all years and designs kept the same allocations and extended the randomized designs to $R=400$ outcomes per allocation, keeping $R=1,000$ for single-assignment designs. The refinement is not a new allocation sample. The rank of the fitted design matrix, residual treatment information, aliased columns, warnings, invalid estimates or intervals, and estimates of $\rho$ at the boundary of its parameter space were recorded. Section
\ref{app:nc} gives the reproducibility and precision details.


\subsection{Reproducibility and Monte Carlo precision}\label{app:nc}


The annual results give equal weight to the six queen parameter pairs
and are reported without pooled Monte Carlo intervals. An independent
check recomputed performance from the simulated trial fits, verified
annual treatment budgets and regional orderings, and confirmed that the
tail refinement reused the main run's assignments and outcomes.

\iffullsi
For randomized designs, the prespecified refinement schedule allowed $J=100,200,400$ and $R=100,200,400,1000,2000,4000$. It extended whichever source of Monte Carlo error, allocations or outcomes, contributed more to the shortfall from the precision targets. For designs with a single possible assignment, only outcomes were extended. If
an allocation occurs \(n_a\) times among \(J\) draws, its risk estimate
enters the mean with weight \(n_a/J\). Therefore, the variance due to outcome noise involves the squared weights \((n_a/J)^2\), because repeated draws of one allocation share one risk estimate. Bias and coverage use the same accounting of frequencies and covariances. The overall precision includes both variation across assignments and outcome noise given an assignment. It distinguishes a design whose sampled assignments happened to coincide from one with only one possible assignment.
\fi

 Approximate Monte Carlo standard errors of setting-specific differences between designs treat the designs' random draws as independent, whereas averages across settings or models do not assume that shared allocations are independent.

We report the variance of the estimated allocation risks before and after
subtracting the contribution of outcome Monte Carlo noise. We did not
truncate the corrected estimates at zero, and none was negative in the
North Carolina refinement. The estimated risks used for quantiles and
worst-decile means still contain outcome noise.

We checked for selection bias by selecting the worst decile with one half
of the simulated outcomes, evaluating it with the other half, then
exchanging the roles of the halves. The resulting reductions relative to
SRS were similar to or slightly larger than the reductions reported from
all outcomes. The Jaccard index measures agreement between the two
sampled worst deciles. It divides the number of assignments in both
deciles by the number in either. It describes how stable sampled tail membership is. With only one possible assignment, the index equals one by
construction and carries no information.


\subsection{Partial participation and sample size}\label{partial-participation-methods}


All 58 colleges will be invited to the proposed extension. To allow for
incomplete participation, we repeated the case study for
$N \in \{15,20,25,29,30,35,40,45,50,58\}$ participating colleges
under the education outcome model with $\tau=0$. Under random
volunteering, we drew 50 subsets for each $N$ and used those same
subsets for all designs, SRS and planning years. At $N=20$ and $N=29$
we also examined volunteering probabilities that increased linearly
with incidence rank. The highest-burden college was three times as
likely to join as the lowest. We reapplied each assignment rule on the
participants and required at least two colleges in each saturation region.

Spatial dependence links participating colleges only. A participant with
no participating neighbors has a zero row in $\mathbf{W}_P$. Spillover
exposure is the number of treated participating neighbors divided by the
number of geographic neighbors,
$e_j(\mathbf{Z})=\sum_{k\in\mathcal{N}(j)}Z_k/|\mathcal{N}(j)|$.
A neighbor that does not participate counts as untreated.

We set $\psi=\kappa\beta$ with $\kappa \in \{0.5,0.8\}$ and used nine
values of $\beta$ from 0 to 2 in units of the college-level residual
standard deviation before spatial propagation. We crossed these settings with $\rho \in \{0,0.5\}$,
both regimes, the eight designs and SRS, and four planning years. With
fewer than 58 colleges, each of the 50 subsets used 20 assignments and
10 outcomes per assignment (10,000 fits per setting). A design with a
single possible assignment on a subset used 200 outcomes for that
assignment. With 58 colleges, randomized rules used 100 assignments and 100
outcomes per assignment. A rule with one possible assignment used
1,000 outcomes for that assignment. The resulting MSE and coverage
matched the main analysis within Monte Carlo error.

We used the same subsets, assignments and noise across effect sizes
to reduce Monte Carlo noise in the comparisons. For each setting we
estimated power with the corrected $t$ test. From the power curves we
obtained the number of colleges needed for 80\% power and the minimum
detectable effect (MDE), the smallest effect detectable with 80\% power
at each $N$.

For planning, suppose the education outcome is a score measured on each trained professional and averaged within each college. We represent a professional's score as a college effect plus individual variation, with total variance 1, so that effects are in individual standard-deviation units. The intraclass correlation coefficient (ICC) is the share of that variance that lies between colleges, so the between-college variance is the ICC and the within-college variance is $1 - \text{ICC}$. Averaging the scores of $n_c$ professionals leaves the college part unchanged and divides the variance of the individual part by $n_c$, so the college mean has standard deviation $\sigma_c = \sqrt{\text{ICC} + (1 - \text{ICC})/n_c}$ \citep{donner_design_2000, hayes_cluster_2017}. Therefore, a structural effect of $\beta$ college-level residual standard deviations, before spatial propagation, corresponds to an individual-level standardized effect $d = \beta\sigma_c$. This treats the sampling error of each college mean as the residual $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ in Equation~\ref{eq:nc-education}, which the spatial multiplier also propagates when $\rho > 0$. With $\tau = 0$, multiplying the effect and the noise by the same constant leaves $\hat\beta/\widehat{\mathrm{SE}}$ unchanged, so power for any $n_c$, ICC and $d$ follows from the simulated college-level results. Rerunning one setting with the effect and the noise both halved reproduced its estimates to within $6\times10^{-8}$. In a sensitivity analysis, the number of trained professionals grew with college population ($n_c$ averaging 25 over participants, ICC $= 0.05$), so the noise variance differed between colleges while the fitted model stayed unweighted, as an analyst unaware of the difference would fit it.


\subsection{Sensitivity analyses}\label{further-sensitivity-analyses}


\iffullsi
We compared the eight designs and SRS with the Friedman, Nemenyi and Holm-adjusted Wilcoxon tests used on the grid, treating the 24 combinations of year, $\rho$ and $\psi$ as blocks, separately for each spillover regime and each value of $\tau$. The four years share one map, and some designs and SRS reuse their simulations across years (Section \ref{outcome-model-and-monte-carlo-evaluation}). Therefore, these $p$-values describe how consistently the designs ranked across blocks that share data.
\fi

To check that the design comparison does not depend on the spatial model, we generated data at $N = 58$ with $\tau = 0$ from the spatial lag model and fit a conditional autoregressive (CAR) model \citep{besag_spatial_1974, cressie_statistics_1993} and the spatial lag model to those data. We also generated data from a CAR model with the same linear predictor and fit both models. We also fit two Bayesian models to the spatial lag data, a CAR model with the prior of Leroux and colleagues \citep{leroux_estimation_2000, lee_carbayes_2013} and a Bayesian spatial lag model \citep{lesage_introduction_2009}. Finally, we replaced the proportional spillover exposure by a binary indicator of any treated neighbor and by a count of treated neighbors scaled by the mean number of neighbors, with the same form in the outcome model and the fit.

\sihead{Estimation models}
The two spatial model families use the linear predictor $\boldsymbol{\mu} = \alpha \mathbf{1} + \beta \mathbf{Z} + \psi\,\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{Z})$, where $\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{Z})$ is the proportional spillover covariate of the main analysis, and use the 58-college queen geography. The spatial lag model of the main analysis is $\mathbf{Y} = \rho \mathbf{W} \mathbf{Y} + \boldsymbol{\mu} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$, $\boldsymbol{\varepsilon} \sim N(\mathbf{0}, \sigma^2 \mathbf{I})$. The CAR model is $\mathbf{Y} = \boldsymbol{\mu} + \mathbf{u}$, $\mathbf{u} \sim N\{\mathbf{0}, \sigma^2 (\mathbf{I} - \lambda \mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{sym}})^{-1}\}$, where $\mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{sym}}$ is the symmetric matrix similar to the row-standardized $\mathbf{W}$ \citep{ord_estimation_1975}, fit by maximum likelihood with the \texttt{spautolm} function of the R package \texttt{spatialreg} \citep{bivand_r_2022}. Thus the spatial lag outcome has conditional mean $(\mathbf{I}-\rho\mathbf{W})^{-1}\boldsymbol{\mu}$ given the assignment, whereas the CAR outcome has conditional mean $\boldsymbol{\mu}$. For the reverse comparison, data were generated as $\mathbf{Y} = \boldsymbol{\mu} + \mathbf{L}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}$ with $\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^\top = (\mathbf{I} - \rho \mathbf{W}_{\mathrm{sym}})^{-1}$ and the same values of $\rho$ as the spatial lag data. These runs used four years, $\rho \in \{0, 0.2, 0.5\}$, $\psi \in \{0.5, 0.8\}$, both regimes, the eight designs and SRS, with 500 fits per setting.

The Bayesian CAR model \citep{leroux_estimation_2000} is $Y_j = \mu_j + \phi_j + e_j$, $e_j \sim N(0, \nu^2)$, with
\begin{equation*}
\phi_j \mid \phi_{-j} \sim N\left(\frac{\lambda \sum_k a_{jk}\phi_k}{\lambda \sum_k a_{jk} + 1 - \lambda},\; \frac{\sigma_\phi^2}{\lambda \sum_k a_{jk} + 1 - \lambda}\right),
\end{equation*}
where $a_{jk}$ is the binary queen adjacency. We used the defaults of the \texttt{S.CARleroux} function of the R package \texttt{CARBayes} \citep{lee_carbayes_2013}, version 6.1.1: independent $N(0, 10^5)$ priors for the regression coefficients, inverse-gamma(1, 0.01) priors for $\nu^2$ and $\sigma_\phi^2$, and a uniform(0, 1) prior for $\lambda$. Each chain ran 25,000 iterations, of which the first 5,000 were discarded, thinned by 5. At $\rho = 0.5$, where fewer than half the chains met the diagnostics for $\beta$, we reran the same data with 100,000 iterations, 20,000 discarded and thinning by 20. The Bayesian spatial lag model \citep{lesage_introduction_2009} has the same form as the maximum likelihood model, with the defaults of the \texttt{spBreg\_lag} function of \texttt{spatialreg}, version 1.4.3: a $N(\mathbf{0}, 10^{12} \mathbf{I})$ prior for the regression coefficients, the prior $p(\sigma^2) \propto 1/\sigma^2$, a Beta(1.01, 1.01) prior for $\rho$ rescaled to its feasible interval, and 10,000 draws of which the first 2,000 were discarded. Both Bayesian models used four years, $\rho \in \{0, 0.5\}$, $\psi = 0.8$, both regimes, the eight designs and SRS, with 100 fits per setting (14,400 fits per model), and both used the same data as the maximum likelihood model. We report the posterior mean of $\beta$ and the 95\% equal-tailed credible interval. A chain met the diagnostics for $\beta$ when its effective sample size of $\beta$ was at least 400 and the absolute Geweke $z$ statistic \citep{geweke_evaluating_1992} was below 1.96.

\sihead{Spillover form}
Under the binary form, $\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{Z}) = \mathbb{I}\{\text{any treated queen neighbor}\}$. Under the count form, $\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{Z}) = (1/\bar d) \times$ (number of treated queen neighbors), where $\bar d = 4.93$ is the mean number of queen neighbors, so a college of average degree with every neighbor treated has $\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{Z}) = 1$ and receives $\psi$, as under the proportional form. Under control-only spillover, both the binary and the count exposure are multiplied by $\mathbf{1}-\mathbf{Z}$.

The spillover regime was the one factor that changed the queen ranking
(\iffullsi Table \ref{tab:regime}, Figure \ref{fig:mse}\else Table~\ref{M-tab:grid-srs} of the article\fi). Under the both-arms
regime, Balanced Quartiles had the lowest MSE (0.042), followed by
Incidence-Guided Saturation (0.046), \(2\times2\) Blocking
(0.062), Checkerboard (0.062), Isolation Buffer (0.161), and High
Incidence Focus (0.162). Under the control-only regime, the order was
Incidence-Guided Saturation (0.135), Isolation Buffer (0.159),
Balanced Quartiles (0.219), High Incidence Focus (0.329), \(2\times2\)

so \(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{Z} = 0\) at every treated cluster and \(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{Z} \circ (\mathbf{1} - \mathbf{Z}) = \mathbf{W}\mathbf{Z}\).

and 0.159, differ only by Monte Carlo error because the two regimes use
separate draws. Every other design had a higher MSE under the
control-only regime, and the increase was largest for Checkerboard and
\(2\times2\) Blocking, which went from close to the best designs under
the both-arms regime to the two worst under the control-only regime.
A trial team may not know in advance which regime applies. Among the six detailed designs, Incidence-Guided Saturation was first or second under both regimes, at this and every other value of $\beta$. However, in the full benchmark that includes SRS (\iffullsi Table \ref{tab:grid-srs}\else Table~\ref{M-tab:grid-srs} of the article\fi), its both-arms MSE was 8\% above that of SRS, a small but statistically detectable difference (Figure \ref{fig:vs-srs}).

\section{Formal statistical comparisons}\label{formal-statistical-comparison}\label{app:supp-comparisons}


The Friedman test rejected equal MSE ranks across the six designs and SRS under
queen contiguity at \(\beta = 1\) (\(\chi^2 = 560.33\),
\(\text{df} = 6\), 160 blocks, \(p < 2.2\times10^{-16}\)). The average
within-block ranks were 1.78 for Incidence-Guided Saturation, 2.60 for SRS,
2.69 for Balanced Quartiles, 4.19 for Isolation Buffer, 5.24 for
\(2\times2\) Blocking, 5.74 for Checkerboard, and 5.76 for High
Incidence Focus. Nemenyi comparisons (critical difference 0.712)
separated 17 of the 21 pairs\iffullsi\ (Figure \ref{fig:cd} shows
the critical-difference diagrams)\fi. Incidence-Guided Saturation
ranked significantly better than every other design and than SRS (\(p = 0.011\)). SRS and Balanced
Quartiles could not be distinguished from each other (\(p = 1.00\)), and both ranked significantly
better than the remaining four designs. The other three pairs that Nemenyi comparisons
did not separate all lay among the bottom three designs: \(2\times2\)
Blocking against High Incidence Focus (\(p = 0.32\)) and Checkerboard (\(p = 0.39\)), and Checkerboard
against High Incidence Focus (\(p = 1.00\)). Holm-adjusted Wilcoxon
signed-rank tests separated 20 of the 21 pairs, all except Balanced Quartiles against SRS (\(p = 0.15\)).

The bottom three designs were ordered differently by average rank and by
mean MSE. By mean MSE the order was High Incidence Focus (0.245),
\(2\times2\) Blocking (0.319), and Checkerboard (1.087). By average rank
it was \(2\times2\) Blocking (5.24), Checkerboard (5.74), and High
Incidence Focus (5.76). Both reversals have the same source: the MSEs of
\(2\times2\) Blocking and Checkerboard depended strongly on the
spillover regime. Each had a lower MSE than High Incidence Focus in all
80 both-arms blocks (mean MSE 0.062 for each, against 0.162) and a
higher MSE in all 80 control-only blocks (0.576 and 2.111, against
0.329; see Section \ref{sensitivity-to-spatial-dependence-spillover-magnitude-and-spillover-regime}). Average rank counts each
block once, so their wins under the both-arms regime offset their losses
under the control-only regime. Mean MSE weights those losses by their
size.

We also compared each pair adjacent in the mean-MSE order, with SRS in
its place, over the 50 independent configuration-by-surface units. Every
adjacent pair differed significantly except SRS and Balanced Quartiles.
The mean paired differences in MSE, with \(t_{49}\) 95\% intervals, were
\(-0.038\) (\(-0.040\), \(-0.035\)) for Incidence-Guided Saturation
against SRS, \(-0.002\) (\(-0.005\), \(0.001\)) for SRS against
Balanced Quartiles (Wilcoxon \(p = 0.15\); SRS lower in 29 of the 50
units), \(-0.029\) (\(-0.033\), \(-0.026\)) for Balanced Quartiles
against Isolation Buffer, \(-0.085\) (\(-0.095\), \(-0.075\)) for
Isolation Buffer against High Incidence Focus, \(-0.074\) (\(-0.086\),
\(-0.062\)) for High Incidence Focus against \(2\times2\) Blocking, and
\(-0.768\) (\(-0.789\), \(-0.746\)) for \(2\times2\) Blocking against
Checkerboard, with Wilcoxon \(p \le 4.9\times10^{-9}\) in every other case.
The better design of each of those pairs had the lower MSE in all 50 units, except
High Incidence Focus against \(2\times2\) Blocking, where it did so in
48 (96\%). The adjacent-pair test separated High Incidence Focus
from \(2\times2\) Blocking, which the Nemenyi comparison of within-block
ranks did not. Each unit's MSE pools the two regimes, and in that pooled
mean the control-only losses of \(2\times2\) Blocking outweigh its
both-arms wins.

\section{\texorpdfstring{Estimation error and its dependence on \(\beta\)}{Estimation error and its dependence on beta}}\label{app:technical}\label{app:technical-notes}\label{estimation-error-and-its-dependence-on-tau}


Section \ref{stability-across-treatment-effect-sizes} reports that
Incidence-Guided Saturation's bias\iffullsi\ and rook Checkerboard's coverage grow\else\ grows\fi\
with \(\beta\). To examine why, we reran 2.4 million oracle fits for
the six designs, both neighbor types and all five values of \(\beta\).
We recorded \(\hat\rho\) and each fit's standard error. The rerun
reproduced all 9,600 scenario summaries. Every number below that involves
\(\hat\rho\), the standard error of \(\hat\beta\) or a term of Equation
\ref{eq:error-decomposition} comes from the rerun. The other numbers come
from the original simulations.

For one fit \((k, j)\), write \(\mathbf{Y}\), \(\mathbf{Z}\), and \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\) for
\(\mathbf{Y}_{kj}\), \(\mathbf{Z}_{kj}\), and \(\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}_{kj}\) (Section \ref{simulation-design-resampling-and-metrics}). Let \(\mathbf{V} = [\mathbf{1}, \mathbf{Z}, \mathbf{S}(\mathbf{Z}), \mathbf{X}_k]\) be the
\(100 \times 4\) covariate matrix of the oracle model, so that Equation
\ref{eq:outcome} reads \((\mathbf{I} - \rho \mathbf{W})\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\theta} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\) with
\(\boldsymbol{\theta} = (0, \beta, \psi, \tau)^\top\). Let \(\mathbf{A} = (\mathbf{I} - \rho \mathbf{W})^{-1}\) and
\(\mathbf{V}^{+} = (\mathbf{V}^\top \mathbf{V})^{-1}\mathbf{V}^\top\), and for a vector \(\mathbf{v}\) write
\([\mathbf{V}^{+}\mathbf{v}]_Z\) for the coefficient on \(\mathbf{Z}\) when \(\mathbf{v}\) is regressed on
the columns of \(\mathbf{V}\) by least squares. Given \(\hat\rho\), the estimated
coefficients are \(\mathbf{V}^{+}(\mathbf{I} - \hat\rho \mathbf{W})\mathbf{Y}\) (Section \ref{estimation}). Because
\((\mathbf{I} - \hat\rho \mathbf{W})\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\theta} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon} + (\rho - \hat\rho)\mathbf{W}\mathbf{Y}\) and
\(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{Y} = \mathbf{W}\mathbf{A}(\mathbf{V}\boldsymbol{\theta} + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon})\), \begin{equation}
\hat\beta - \beta = [\mathbf{V}^{+}\boldsymbol{\varepsilon}]_Z + (\rho - \hat\rho)\,h + \beta(\rho - \hat\rho)\,g,
\label{eq:error-decomposition}
\end{equation} where \[
g = [\mathbf{V}^{+}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Z}]_Z, \qquad h = [\mathbf{V}^{+}\mathbf{W}\mathbf{A}\{\psi\,\mathbf{S}(\mathbf{Z}) + \tau \mathbf{X}_k + \boldsymbol{\varepsilon}\}]_Z.
\] The first term has mean zero given \(\mathbf{Z}\) and \(\mathbf{X}_k\). The last term
has a factor of \(\beta\). It is present exactly when \(g \neq 0\),
where \(g\) is the coefficient on \(\mathbf{Z}\) when the lagged treatment
\(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Z} = \mathbf{W}\mathbf{Z} + \rho \mathbf{W}^2 \mathbf{Z} + \rho^2 \mathbf{W}^3 \mathbf{Z} + \cdots\) is regressed on the
model's own covariates, and its sign and size are set by the error in
\(\hat\rho\). The concentrated likelihood depends on \(\mathbf{Y}\) only through
the residual sum of squares of \((\mathbf{I} - \tilde\rho \mathbf{W})\mathbf{Y}\) on \(\mathbf{V}\), and the
part of that residual that involves \(\beta\) is
\(\beta(\rho - \tilde\rho)\) times the residual of \(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Z}\) on \(\mathbf{V}\). If
\(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{A}\mathbf{Z}\) lies in the column space of \(\mathbf{V}\), that part vanishes for every
\(\tilde\rho\), so \(\hat\rho\) is the same at every value of \(\beta\);
if in addition \(g = 0\), then \(\hat\beta - \beta\) is the same at every
value of \(\beta\). Equation \ref{eq:error-decomposition} held on every
re-run fit, with a largest residual of \(4.2\times10^{-13}\)\iffullsi; for
Checkerboard under rook contiguity it held in the reduced form given
below\else. For Checkerboard under rook contiguity, whose spillover column is aliased, it held with that column dropped\fi.

Under queen contiguity, pooled over the 160 blocks, the bias of
Incidence-Guided Saturation rose from 0.029 at \(\beta=0.8\) to 0.047
at \(\beta=3\) (Section \ref{stability-across-treatment-effect-sizes}).
The bias of Isolation Buffer stayed between 0.002 and 0.004. The last
term of Equation \ref{eq:error-decomposition} accounted for almost all
of the rise for Incidence-Guided Saturation.

The estimate \(\hat\rho\) was biased downward by about 0.06 on average.
For Incidence-Guided Saturation, the downward bias was 0.071 at
\(\beta=0.8\) and 0.054 at \(\beta=3\). Finite-sample bias of
\(\hat\rho\) is known in spatial autoregressive models
\citep{bao_finite-sample_2013}. The last term depends on the product
\((\rho-\hat\rho)g\) as well as on \(\beta\). For Incidence-Guided
Saturation, \(g\) averaged 0.371 over the queen fits. The last term
contributed 0.021 and 0.043 to bias at the two effect sizes. The first
term contributed 0.001 at both values, and the second contributed
0.007 and 0.002. For Isolation Buffer, \(g\) averaged 0.020 and the
last term contributed 0.001 and 0.004.

Most of the difference in
\(g\) comes from the control-only regime. Under the both-arms regime
with \(\rho = 0\), the lagged treatment is \(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{Z} = \mathbf{S}(\mathbf{Z})\), one of
the covariates, so \(g = 0\) for every design. Under the control-only
regime, \(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{Z} = \mathbf{S}(\mathbf{Z}) + \mathbf{Z} \circ \mathbf{W}\mathbf{Z}\), and at \(\rho = 0\), \(g\) is
close to the average share of treated neighbors among treated clusters.
That share is 0.57 for Incidence-Guided Saturation, whose
treated clusters are concentrated in the high-saturation quadrants, and
exactly 0 for Isolation Buffer, whose treated clusters have no treated
neighbors. For Isolation Buffer, \(\mathbf{Z} \circ \mathbf{W}\mathbf{Z} = \mathbf{0}\), so the two regimes
define the same spillover covariate and the same \(g\), which is 0 at
\(\rho = 0\) and about 0.06 at \(\rho = 0.5\); at \(\rho = 0\), its
\(\hat\rho\) and its estimation error were the same at every value of
\(\beta\), up to the optimizer's tolerance.

The same term explains why the control-only MSE of Incidence-Guided
Saturation rose from 0.131 at \(\beta = 0.8\) to 0.182 at
\(\beta = 3\) while that of Isolation Buffer did not. Under that regime,
averaged over the 80 queen blocks, the variance of the last term of
Equation \ref{eq:error-decomposition} rose from 0.008 to 0.061 for
Incidence-Guided Saturation, while the variance of the rest of
the error changed little (0.119 and 0.115). The variance of the last
term grew more slowly than \(\beta^2\) because \(\hat\rho\) became less
variable as \(\beta\) grew. For Isolation Buffer, the variance of the
last term stayed below 0.0002. The rise in bias slowed at larger values
of \(\beta\) for a related reason: the part of the likelihood that
involves \(\beta\) sharpens it around the true \(\rho\) as \(\beta\)
grows, so the downward bias of \(\hat\rho\) shrank.

\section{Sensitivity of incidence guidance to regions and saturation spread}\label{igs-sensitivity-results}

Figure~\ref{fig:igs-sensitivity} shows the MSE of each variant relative to SRS (Section~\ref{igs-variants-methods}). Pooled over the five values of \(\beta\), SRS had MSE 0.227 under control-only spillover and 0.043 under both-arms spillover. These pooled ratios differ from those at \(\beta = 1\) in Section~\ref{srs-benchmark-and-allocation-specific-risk}. Under control-only spillover, widening the guided saturations from 60/40 to 90/10 lowered MSE for every number of regions, and at each of these spreads fewer, larger regions had lower MSE. At 90/10 the guided ratios to SRS were 0.55 for halves, 0.59 for quadrants, 0.77 for ten regions and 1.05 for 25 blocks, against 0.67 for the original design. Under both-arms spillover, wider spreads raised MSE for halves and quadrants. Guided quadrants rose from 1.00 times SRS at 60/40 to 1.22 at 90/10, and halves from 0.98 to 1.94. With ten regions or 25 blocks, MSE stayed between 1.00 and 1.22 times that of SRS from 70/30 to 100/0. Some variants traded less between the regimes than the original design. Quadrants at 70/30 and ten regions at 80/20 had ratios of 0.84 and 0.89 under control-only spillover and 1.02 under both-arms spillover, where neither differed detectably from SRS.

Moving from 90/10 to the 100/0 boundary had different effects by number of regions under control-only spillover. MSE rose for halves, from 0.55 to 0.88 times that of SRS for the guided version, and the bias of \(\hat\beta\) rose from 0.086 to 0.161. Quadrants changed little (0.59 at both spreads), while MSE fell for ten regions (0.77 to 0.68) and 25 blocks (1.05 to 0.86). At 100/0, both 25-block versions had detectably lower MSE than SRS for the first time. Under both-arms spillover, MSE rose at 100/0 for every number of regions in both versions. The largest rise was for halves, whose MSE reached 8.13 times that of SRS. When one half is fully treated, \(\mathbf{W}\mathbf{Z}\) differs from \(\mathbf{Z}\) only at the 20 clusters along the dividing line, and the two columns have correlation 0.941 under queen contiguity. The model then has full rank but separates treatment from spillover poorly. At 100/0, coverage of the \(t\)-based interval was between 0.932 and 0.953, lowest for halves under control-only spillover. The changes from 90/10 to 100/0 are descriptive, because the tests compare each design with SRS, the original design or its twin.

Incidence guidance changed where treatment went more than how accurate the estimate was. At the original 80/20 quadrants, guided and unguided MSE were close (ratios 1.00 under control-only and 1.04 under both-arms spillover). No guided variant had detectably lower MSE than its unguided twin, and every detectable difference favored the unguided version. At 100/0 under control-only spillover, guided MSE was 1.04 to 1.10 times that of the twin, and all four differences remained after Holm adjustment. In the targeting check (queen contiguity, \(\rho = 0\), \(\psi = 0.5\), control-only spillover, averaged over the five incidence configurations), the original design treated 52.7\% of total incidence. The share grew with the spread and the number of regions, to 57.7\% for 25 blocks at 90/10 and 53.6--60.1\% at 100/0, while the unguided versions and SRS treated about 50\%.

Rook contiguity gave the same directions from 90/10 to 100/0. Both versions of halves at 100/0 had slightly higher MSE than SRS under control-only spillover. Under both-arms spillover, their MSEs were 17.25 and 17.26 times that of SRS for the unguided and guided versions, respectively. With the non-oracle model, MSE fell from 90/10 to 100/0 for quadrants under control-only spillover with both neighbor types, and the both-arms penalty for halves remained, with coverage as low as 0.607 under rook contiguity. Adding the 100/0 variants enlarged the Holm families, and 3 of the 756 earlier comparisons rose above 0.05, all in rook sensitivity analyses. No queen oracle conclusion changed. These results cover equal-size partitions of the grid with linearly spaced targets. The North Carolina map was not part of this study.

\section{Accuracy, inference and reach in the case study}\label{completed-results-accuracy-inference-and-reach}


The main analysis used 7,598,000 distinct simulated outcomes. Every setting
met its precision targets for mean MSE and coverage. The largest relative
Monte Carlo standard error of MSE was 0.049, and the largest Monte Carlo
standard error of coverage was 0.00892. We recorded no warnings,
aliased columns, boundary estimates of $\rho$ or estimation failures.
The tail refinement added 2,280,000 distinct outcomes, giving
9,878,000 distinct outcomes across the two stages.

\iffullsi Figure \ref{fig:nc-mse} compares the eight designs with SRS by year and regime. Setting-specific comparisons are in Figure \ref{fig:nc-setting-ratios}.\else Figure~\ref{M-fig:nc-mse} of the article compares the six designs with SRS by year and regime.\fi\ 
With $\sigma=1$, MSE is in squared college-level residual standard deviations. Under control-only spillover, Saturation Quadrants has descriptive mean MSE
0.223 against SRS 0.308, a 27.4\% reduction. Incidence-Guided Saturation
reduces mean MSE by 21.1--29.9\% across the four observed years (\iffullsi Table
\ref{tab:nc-year}\else Table \ref{tab:nc-annual}\fi). Both saturation designs have lower mean MSE than SRS in every primary parameter setting, and approximate Monte Carlo intervals for each setting-specific difference excluded zero. The two had similar accuracy, and their advantage over SRS comes from the saturation structure they share (Section \ref{app:variants}). Under control-only spillover, the accuracy of High Incidence Focus
depended on the planning year.

Under both-arms spillover, SRS mean MSE is 0.076, Saturation Quadrants
0.075, and Incidence-Guided Saturation ranges from 0.074 to 0.077 across
years. SRS remains competitive with balancing, blocking and saturation,
and small point differences do not establish equivalence. High Incidence
Focus has low both-arms MSE in the primary model, but its allocation is
deterministic for these fixed rates. Its low MSE describes model-based accuracy for that one allocation. With only one possible allocation, its validity rests on the outcome model alone.

Mean coverage of the 95\% intervals across the four years was between 94.1\% and 94.9\% across the eight designs and SRS in both regimes, and between 94.1\% and 95.1\% in single years. The Wald interval covered only 92.6\% to 93.6\% (Table \ref{tab:interval-comparison}). Section \ref{interval-calibration} compares the corrected $t$ interval with the Wald interval and other alternatives. Table \ref{tab:nc-annual} gives bias and coverage for every design, year and regime. Checkerboard's treatment effect is identifiable on this irregular graph, but weak separation of treatment from spillover makes its estimates imprecise. On the rook grid, by contrast, Checkerboard's treatment effect is not identifiable (Section \ref{identification-diagnostics}).

Population shares put these results in context. High Incidence Focus treats
29 clusters but only about 22--24\% of working-age population, while Isolation Buffer treats a mean of 15.89 clusters and about 27\%. These population denominators measure geographic reach only. $2\times2$ Blocking treats 28, 31, 27 and 29 clusters by year.
Table \ref{tab:nc-budgets}\iffullsi\ and Figure \ref{fig:nc-pop} give\else\ gives\fi\ the complete
budgets and shares.

Incidence-Guided Saturation sends more treatment to high-incidence
regions when incidence differs between regions. In North Carolina it
differs little, because the four regions' mean incidence ranks lay
between 24 and 34 of 58 in every year. With all 58 colleges, the treated
colleges held on average 51.1\% of the summed college incidence rates
under Incidence-Guided Saturation, 50.4\% under Saturation Quadrants,
50.0\% under SRS and 62.2\% under High Incidence Focus. Their shares of
deaths ran the other way, at 49.2\%, 49.6\%, 49.7\% and 35.4\%, because
the highest-rate colleges serve small populations. On the grid, where
regional incidence differed more and equal cluster populations make rate
share and death share coincide, incidence guidance raised the treated
share of incidence over Saturation Quadrants by 2.1 to 3.1 percentage
points on average across the five incidence configurations, and High
Incidence Focus treated 75\% to 77\% of total incidence by giving up
randomization over the whole map. Figure \ref{fig:spread-gain} shows that
the gain grows with the spread of regional mean ranks and places the
North Carolina regions at the low end of that spread. Section
\ref{igs-sensitivity-results} shows how the number of regions and the
saturation targets affect targeting and MSE on the grid.

\subsection{Colleges needed and power}\label{planning-for-realistic-participation}


Under both-arms spillover, the design hardly affected the number of
colleges needed. With $\rho=0.5$ and $\psi=0.8\beta$, the estimated
numbers for 80\% power were 35 to 37 colleges at an effect of one
college-level residual standard deviation and about 19 colleges at 1.5
standard deviations, except for Checkerboard and Isolation Buffer
(Figure \ref{fig:colleges-needed}, Table \ref{tab:nc-power}). At
$\beta=1$, those two designs did not reach 80\% power with all 58
colleges.

Under control-only spillover, no design reached 80\% power at $\beta = 1$ with all 58 colleges. At $\beta = 1.5$ the two saturation designs needed 28 colleges, against 29 to 32 for SRS and the rank-based designs (High Incidence Focus, Balanced Quartiles and Balanced Halves), while $2\times2$ Blocking and Isolation Buffer failed to reach 80\% power with 58 colleges in one of the four planning years. Power under control-only spillover rose slowly beyond about 40 colleges\iffullsi\ (Figure \ref{fig:power-n})\fi.\iffullsi\ In Figure \ref{fig:power-n} the two saturation designs nearly coincide, and the year-to-year range is narrower than the lines except near 58 colleges.\fi\ Because each number of colleges uses its own participant subsets, the small drop in power from 29 to 30 colleges reflects which subsets were drawn. When nearly every college participates, nearly every control has a treated neighbor, so spillover exposure varies little and is hard to separate from treatment. Non-participating neighbors restore some of that variation, and so do the saturation designs. With all 58 colleges, the smallest effect detectable with 80\% power was 0.76 to 0.78 college-level residual standard deviations under both-arms spillover for every design except Checkerboard (1.32) and Isolation Buffer (1.50), and 1.24 to 1.25 for the two saturation designs under control-only spillover, against 1.45 to 1.57 for SRS and the rank-based designs.

When the number of trained professionals varies, with $n_c$ proportional to college population and averaging 25, and ICC $= 0.05$, power fell by 0.097 on average across designs, settings and effect sizes. The Wald test rejected a true null hypothesis in 11\% to 13\% of simulated trials with 15 colleges and in 6\% to 7\% with 58. With the corrected $t$ test the rejection rate was 4.4\% to 5.6\%, and the coverage of the corrected interval at $\beta = 1$ was between 94.0\% and 95.6\% for every design and regime at every number of colleges (Table \ref{tab:nc-calibration}). Therefore, the sample-size estimates use a test with a rejection rate close to the nominal 5\% level in these simulations.

\subsection{Sensitivity analyses}\label{nc-sensitivity-results}


The design comparison held when spillover depended on the number of
treated neighbors rather than their share, and in most spatial-model
comparisons. The exceptions were CAR
models fit to spatial lag data. The maximum likelihood CAR fit was
biased under strong spatial dependence, and the Bayesian CAR fit did not
reproduce the saturation designs' control-only advantage. When spillover depended only on whether any neighbor was treated,
whether $\beta$ was identifiable depended on the assignment.\iffullsi\
Formal rank tests summarize the ordering of the eight designs and SRS
within each regime.\fi

\sihead{Spatial model}
Fitting a CAR model, generating the data from a CAR model, or both, left the saturation designs with 0.76 to 0.82 times SRS's MSE under control-only spillover, and Checkerboard and Isolation Buffer the worst designs under both-arms spillover. When the CAR model was fit to spatial lag data by maximum likelihood, its estimates were biased by 0.035 and 0.085 at $\rho = 0.2$ and by 0.110 and 0.205 at $\rho = 0.5$ under both-arms and control-only spillover. When it was fit to CAR data, its bias was at most 0.005. Under the spatial lag model the outcome mean includes the spatial multiplier. Therefore, the CAR coefficient on treatment estimates a different quantity from $\beta$ \citep{wall_close_2004}.

\sihead{Bayesian CAR model}
With the longer chains, 83.1\% of the Bayesian CAR chains at $\rho=0.5$ met the diagnostics for $\beta$, compared with 46.2\% of the shorter chains, but 12.5\% still had an absolute Geweke $z$ of at least 1.96, against about 5\% expected by chance. Among chains that met those diagnostics, the Bayesian CAR estimate was biased by $+0.138$ under both-arms spillover and $+0.219$ under control-only spillover, with credible-interval coverage of 90.4\% and 87.7\%. Across all maximum likelihood fits to the same datasets, the biases were $+0.023$ and $+0.028$ and the corrected $t$ coverage 95.0\% and 94.6\%. The Bayesian CAR model also did not reproduce the saturation designs' control-only advantage. Its bias has the same source as that of the maximum likelihood CAR fit to spatial lag data, and the Bayesian spatial lag model uses the same spatial multiplier as the simulated outcome.

\sihead{Bayesian spatial lag model}
Of its chains, 94.9\% met the diagnostics for $\beta$. The median effective sample size of $\beta$ was 8,000 (minimum 5,897) and 5.1\% had an absolute Geweke $z$ of at least 1.96. Its posterior means correlated 0.999 with the maximum likelihood estimates (mean absolute difference 0.017). Its bias was at most 0.039 in absolute value in every combination of $\rho$ and regime, and its credible intervals covered 94.6\% to 95.3\% over all chains (94.5\% to 95.3\% over chains meeting the diagnostics). The Spearman correlation of its design ordering with that of maximum likelihood averaged 0.96 to 1.00 across the four combinations of $\rho$ and regime (0.83 in the lowest single block), and the best design agreed in every block. \iffullsi Tables \ref{tab:nc-car} and \ref{tab:nc-bayes} give each strategy's MSE relative to SRS under each model.\else Table \ref{tab:nc-car} gives the MSE of each design relative to SRS under each combination of data-generating and fitted model.\fi

\sihead{Formal tests on the case study results}
Friedman tests over the 24 combinations of year, $\rho$ and $\psi$ rejected equal ranks of the eight designs and SRS in all four combinations of regime and $\tau$ ($\chi^2 = 135.3$, 153.8, 150.7 and 179.8 on 8 degrees of freedom, $p \le 2.3\times10^{-25}$; Nemenyi critical difference 2.45). Under control-only spillover, Saturation Quadrants and Incidence-Guided Saturation had the two best average ranks (1.67 and 1.79 with $\tau = 0$; 1.29 and 1.71 with $\tau = 1$), and SRS ranked seventh with $\tau = 0$ and sixth with $\tau = 1$. Under both-arms spillover with $\tau = 0$, High Incidence Focus ranked first (1.88) and SRS sixth (5.17), with the balancing and saturation designs between them. These tests treat the 24 blocks as independent, although the four years share one map and some designs and SRS reuse their simulations across years (Section \ref{further-sensitivity-analyses}). Therefore, they describe how consistent the ordering is.

\iffullsi
\subsection{Implications for trial planning}\label{nc-planning-implications}


The case study supports considering either saturation strategy when
control-only spillover is plausible, and SRS alongside other randomized
strategies when both-arms exposure is expected. A trial team should
consider likely spillover, recruitment, cluster and participant budgets,
achievable measurement precision and interval calibration before
selecting one realized allocation. The findings do not establish
intervention effectiveness or a final trial protocol.
